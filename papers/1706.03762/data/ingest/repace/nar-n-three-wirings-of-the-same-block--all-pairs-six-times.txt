TITLE: The encoder: an all-pairs mixer, six times over

INTRO:
One level up gave the shape of the layer. This one gives the tensors and the bill: what an encoder layer actually multiplies, what kind of claim Table 1's path-length row is, how a representation differs between layer 1 and layer 5, and how much the three appendix pictures establish.

--- CHAPTER id=one-layer-in-tensors ---
TITLE: One layer, in tensors

One layer, one head. The input is the previous layer's output: $n$ rows of width [[d-model|512]]. Three learned matrices $W^Q, W^K, W^V \in \mathbb{R}^{512 \times 64}$ project every row down to [[dk-dv-dimensions|64]] — [[queries-keys-values|queries, keys and values]], all three read off the same rows, which is what makes it [[encoder-self-attention|self-attention]]. $QK^{T}$ is the $n \times n$ block. Divide by $\sqrt{d_k} = 8$ ([[sqrt-dk-scaling]]), [[softmax]] each row, multiply by $V$, and $n \times 64$ comes back out.

[[num-heads-h|Eight]] of those run at once on their own projections, concatenate to $n \times 512$, and pass through one more matrix $W^O \in \mathbb{R}^{512 \times 512}$ ([[eq-multihead]]). The head width is not chosen independently: $d_k = d_v = d_{model}/h$, set so that eight narrow heads cost about what one full-width head would. There is no mask, no truncation, no sub-sampling anywhere in the encoder — every row of every score matrix runs across the whole source.

Concretely: the sentence drawn in [[fig-3]] is padded out to 33 positions. That is 1089 scores per head, 8712 per layer, and across [[n-layers|six]] layers roughly 52,000 query-key products for one short sentence, one direction, one training step. [[fig-2]] is the same path as a block diagram.

Worth noticing what the arithmetic is actually dominated by at that size. The $O(n^{2} \cdot d)$ figure the paper quotes for a self-attention layer counts the scoring and the blending. The projections that produce $Q$, $K$, $V$ and the output projection are per-position matrix multiplies of shape $O(n \cdot d^{2})$ — the same shape as a recurrent layer's — and at $n = 33$, $d = 512$ they are the larger term. The table does not break them out.

--- CHAPTER id=what-constant-path-costs ---
TITLE: What the constant path buys, and what it costs

[[table-1]]'s path-length row is why this wiring is in the paper at all, so read what kind of claim it is. It gives [[maximum-path-length|maximum path length]] $O(1)$ for a self-attention layer against $O(n)$ for a [[recurrent-neural-network|recurrent]] one, and [[minimum-sequential-operations|sequential operations]] $O(1)$ against $O(n)$. Those are hop counts in a computation graph, not measurements. The step from short paths to easier learning of [[long-range-dependencies]] is asserted with a citation rather than tested: nothing in the paper swaps a self-attention encoder layer for a recurrent one and reports the difference. [[layer-comparison-desiderata|The three criteria]] are an argument, not an experiment.

The bill is the other column. [[complexity-per-layer]] is $O(n^{2} \cdot d)$ against a recurrent layer's $O(n \cdot d^{2})$, so self-attention is the cheaper of the two exactly when $n < d$. With $d = 512$ and sentences cut into [[word-piece-encoding|word-piece]] or [[byte-pair-encoding|byte-pair]] units, the paper says this is most often the case. That is a claim scoped to sentence-length inputs. Past a few hundred tokens the inequality turns over.

Section 4 names its own escape hatch and then declines to use it. [[restricted-self-attention]] would let each position attend only to a neighborhood of size $r$, cutting the quadratic term but pushing path length back up to $O(n/r)$. The paper reports no model built this way; it plans to investigate it in future work.

There is also a cost the background section admits that no column shows. Collapsing many positions into one weighted average reduces effective resolution ([[attention-averaging-resolution-cost]]). [[multi-head-attention|Running eight heads]] is the stated counter to it — which makes the head count a repair for a known weakness rather than a free extra.

--- CHAPTER id=six-rounds ---
TITLE: Six rounds, and what the stack carries forward

What keeps six identical rounds from being mere repetition is the wrapping. Every [[sub-layer]] outputs $\mathrm{LayerNorm}(x + \mathrm{Sublayer}(x))$ — a [[residual-connection]], then [[layer-normalization]] — so an attention sub-layer never replaces position 7's vector. It adds an increment to it. At the top of layer 3, that vector is the embedding plus six sub-layer increments, normalized six times.

The attention sub-layers are the only place anything crosses between positions. The other one, [[position-wise-feed-forward-network|the feed-forward network]], is [[eq-ffn|$\max(0, xW_1 + b_1)W_2 + b_2$]]: 512 up to $d_{ff} = 2048$, ReLU, back down to 512, applied to each position separately and identically, with its own weights per layer. The paper's alternative description is two convolutions of kernel size 1. So the [[encoder-stack]] alternates — mix across positions, transform each position, six times ([[fig-1]]).

That alternation is what makes attending over the layer below different in kind at depth. In layer 1 the keys are projections of embeddings plus positional signal. In layer 5 they are projections of vectors that have already been mixed across the whole sentence four times and pushed through four ReLU expansions. The dot product is scoring those, not words.

One thing enters exactly once. [[positional-encoding|Position]] is added at the bottom, to the embeddings, and never re-injected, so whatever order information reaches layer 5 has survived six rounds of weighted averaging on the residual path. The paper does not check that it does. Nor does it say what any individual layer contributes: the layers are identical in shape and described as identical, and the only one it ever opens is the fifth.

--- CHAPTER id=the-layer-it-opens ---
TITLE: The only layer the paper opens

All three appendix pictures come from the same place: layer 5 of [[n-layers|six]], [[encoder-self-attention|encoder self-attention]], in a trained translation model, drawn as lines from a querying copy of the sentence to an attended copy, with line opacity standing in for weight.

[[fig-3]] draws one query row out of 33 — the verb "making" — with lines colored by [[attention-head|head]]. Blue, orange, green and gray pile onto "more"; blue, green, red and brown onto "difficult", two heads appearing in both groups. Those words sit six and seven tokens later, with "the registration or voting process" in between. A purple head attends to "making" itself, a pink one to "2009" and "laws". The other 32 query rows are not shown.

[[fig-4]] holds the sentence fixed and varies the head. Its top panel is head 5's full map, mostly faint lines. Its bottom panel keeps only what leaves the word "its": head 5 sends nearly all of it to "Law", head 6 nearly all to "application". The caption's operative word is "apparently".

[[fig-5]] is one more sentence, two heads, whole maps. One spreads a haze across nearly every pair with heavy fans converging on a few words; the other is sparse and short-range. Its caption splits in two: that the heads "clearly learned to perform different tasks" is stated flat, while the tie to sentence structure only "seems" to hold.

So: two sentences, one layer, chosen heads, no axes, no printed numbers, no counts over a corpus, opacity as the only quantitative channel. Section 4 files [[attention-interpretability|interpretability]] as a side benefit and hedges to heads that "appear to exhibit" such behavior.

The measured claim about heads sits elsewhere and says less. [[table-3]] varies $h$ with computation held constant: single-head attention comes in [[attention-heads-ablation|0.9 BLEU below the best setting]], and too many heads is worse again. Several heads help. What any one of them does is not in that number, and [[multi-head-attention]] is not otherwise probed.

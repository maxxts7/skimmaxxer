TITLE: The parts that act on one position at a time

INTRO:
The parent named the three pieces and what each is for. This level gives the sizes and the parameter arithmetic behind them, separates what the paper argues from what it imports by citation, and looks at the single row of evidence that was supposed to settle the position question.

--- CHAPTER id=the-widest-part-of-the-layer ---
TITLE: Where the sizes come from, and the one that was never tested

Equation (2) fixes the shape of the block but not its size. Two numbers do that. The input and output width is [[d-model|512]], and it is not a free choice: every [[sub-layer]] is wrapped in a [[residual-connection]], and the addition needs matching shapes. The inner width, [[d-ff|2048]], is the free one. The paper sets it to four times the model width and gives no reason.

Nothing in the paper tests that ratio on its own. In [[table-3]], group (C) moves $d_{ff}$ only in lockstep with $d_{model}$ — 256 paired with 1024, 1024 paired with 4096. What [[size-and-dropout-ablation|those rows show]] is that a bigger model is better overall: halving the width costs 1.3 BLEU, doubling it gains 0.2 while taking the parameter count from 65M to 168M. They say nothing about whether 4:1 is the right proportion.

The block holds a large share of the weights. $W_1$ is 512×2048 and $W_2$ is 2048×512, about 2.1M weights per sub-layer, and there is one in each of the six [[encoder-stack|encoder]] and six [[decoder-stack|decoder]] layers — roughly 25M of the base model's 65M. That is arithmetic on the paper's stated sizes, not a figure it reports.

The paper offers one alternative reading, in a single line: the same computation is "two convolutions with kernel size 1". [[eq-ffn|The equation]] and the convolution view are the same arithmetic, but the convolution phrasing makes the isolation obvious — a width-1 kernel cannot reach a neighbour, so the [[relu|ReLU]] inside the [[position-wise-feed-forward-network]] never sees two positions at once. Weights are shared across positions within a layer and separate across layers: twelve different per-position functions, each applied identically everywhere.

--- CHAPTER id=one-matrix-three-jobs ---
TITLE: The sentence that imports another paper

The paper spends two sentences and one citation on embeddings. Unpacked, that is a large parameter decision.

[[token-embeddings|The embedding]] is a matrix of vocabulary size by 512. On English-German the vocabulary is about 37000 shared [[byte-pair-encoding|byte-pair]] tokens, so one copy holds roughly 19M weights, against a base model of 65M in total. Three separate copies — encoder input, decoder input, pre-[[softmax]] projection — would come to something near 57M by themselves. [[embedding-softmax-weight-sharing|Folding them into one matrix]] is most of the difference between a 65M model and one close to twice that. The paper does not draw the arithmetic out.

Sharing across source and target works only because there is a single vocabulary covering both. That comes from [[joint-bpe-shared-vocabulary|learning the BPE merges jointly]] on the concatenated [[wmt-2014-en-de|English and German text]], a fact stated in Section 5.1 and never connected back to Section 3.4. Without it, the three-way sharing has nothing to share.

The reasoning for tying lives in the cited work. That paper separates [[input-and-output-embeddings|the input embedding from the pre-softmax projection]], notes they have the same shape, and argues for [[weight-tying|using one matrix in both roles]]. Its account of why this helps is about [[tied-embedding-update-dynamics|gradients]]: untied, only the current input token's row gets an input-embedding update on a given step, while every row of the output matrix is touched. [[three-way-weight-tying|The three-way variant]] is the translation case the Transformer takes off the shelf.

One piece carries no stated reason at all. The shared weights are multiplied by [[sqrt-dmodel-embedding-multiplier|$\sqrt{d_{model}}$]], about 22.6, in the embedding layers only. The paper asserts it in half a sentence and moves on. Note where the scaled vectors then go: they are summed with position vectors whose entries are bounded by 1.

--- CHAPTER id=order-added-by-hand ---
TITLE: Order as a bank of frequencies

A recurrent layer gets order from the sequence it runs in; a [[convolutional-sequence-models|convolution]] gets it from kernel offsets. [[self-attention|Self-attention]] has neither, so order has to arrive as data. It enters at one point: the bottom of both stacks, added to the embedding vector rather than concatenated to it. Addition keeps the width at 512 and costs nothing downstream, but position and token content then share the same dimensions and the model has to separate them itself. The paper does not discuss that.

Inside the vector, dimensions pair up. Entry $2i$ is a sine and entry $2i+1$ a cosine of the same argument, $pos/10000^{2i/d_{model}}$. At $i=0$ the divisor is 1 and the wavelength is $2\pi$: that pair cycles every few tokens. Near the top of the range the divisor approaches 10000 and the wavelength stretches toward $10000\cdot 2\pi$, barely turning across a whole sentence. [[eq-posenc|The two formulas]] give each position a fixed signature with fine digits and coarse ones. Nothing in it is trained, so this [[positional-encoding|encoding]] adds no parameters.

The stated motive is a property of that construction. For a fixed offset $k$, the encoding of $pos+k$ is [[relative-position-linearity|a linear function of the encoding of $pos$]] — for each sine/cosine pair, a rotation by a fixed angle that does not depend on $pos$. A linear map could implement "shift by three positions" once and apply it anywhere in the sequence. The paper says it hypothesized this would make attending by relative position easy to learn. It reports no check that any head actually does so.

One training detail belongs here rather than in Section 5: [[residual-dropout|dropout is applied to the embedding-plus-encoding sum]] in both stacks, at rate 0.1 for the base model.

--- CHAPTER id=the-row-that-settled-nothing ---
TITLE: The row that settled nothing

The alternative was tried. [[learned-positional-embedding|Learned positional embeddings]] — a trainable vector per position, the scheme the paper cites alongside its own — appear as row (E) of [[table-3]]: 4.92 perplexity and 25.7 BLEU, against the base model's 4.92 and 25.8.

Read the setup before the numbers. These are [[transformer-ablations|one-knob ablations]] on newstest2013, the English-German development set, decoded with [[beam-search]] but without [[checkpoint-averaging]]. [[perplexity|The perplexity]] is per word-piece, so it is comparable only within the table. Each row is one trained model. A 0.1 [[bleu|BLEU]] gap between two single runs, on a dev set, is not a measurement that separates them.

So the experiment does not choose. The paper picks [[sinusoidal-positional-encoding|sinusoids]] on a ground outside the table: they *may* let the model handle sequences longer than any it trained on. The mechanism behind that word is concrete — the sine and cosine are defined at any $pos$, while a learned table simply has no row for a position it never saw during training. But the ablation ran on the training distribution's lengths, so extrapolation was never in its scope, and no longer-sequence test is reported anywhere in the paper.

The stake is small in both directions. A learned table costs one 512-wide row per position up to some maximum length — a rounding error against 65M parameters — and buys, by the paper's own measurement, nothing. Sinusoids cost nothing and buy an untested possibility. What the section actually establishes is narrower than it first reads: that the model needs *some* position signal at the bottom of the stacks, and that at these sequence lengths two quite different ones perform the same. [[etheme-order-without-recurrence]] follows that thread on its own.

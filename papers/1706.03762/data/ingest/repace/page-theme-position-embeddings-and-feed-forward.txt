Attention mixes information across positions. That leaves two gaps: nothing yet transforms a position's own vector, and nothing tells the model which position a token sits in. These three pieces close both gaps and finish the layer.

### The other half of every layer

In [[fig-1|the model diagram]], each layer is an attention [[sub-layer|sub-layer]] followed by a [[position-wise-feed-forward-network]]. [[eq-ffn|Equation 2]] is the whole thing:

$$\mathrm{FFN}(x) = \max(0, xW_1 + b_1)W_2 + b_2$$

Widen each $d_{model}=512$ vector to [[d-ff|$d_{ff}=2048$]], apply [[relu|ReLU]], project back to 512. The same weights run at every position in a layer; different layers get their own. The paper offers a second reading: two convolutions with kernel size 1.

Note what it is not. It never looks sideways. All cross-position mixing already happened in the attention sub-layer, which produces a weighted average of value vectors — linear in those values. The feed-forward block is where per-position nonlinear transformation lives. The paper does not argue this; it states the shape and moves on. It is also not small: two $512 \times 2048$ matrices per layer, roughly double the parameters of that layer's [[multi-head-attention|attention projections]].

### One matrix doing three jobs

[[embedding-softmax-weight-sharing]] means the encoder's [[token-embeddings|input embedding]], the decoder's input embedding, and the pre-softmax output projection are all the same weights. The paper does this by citation, pointing at [[weight-tying]] rather than restating why tying helps. There is no ablation for it here, so treat it as a parameter-count and convention choice the paper inherited, not something this paper tested.

The one wrinkle is the [[sqrt-dmodel-embedding-multiplier]]: in the embedding role, the shared weights are scaled by $\sqrt{d_{model}} \approx 22.6$. The paper states this in one clause and gives no reason. The usual reading is that a matrix whose scale suits producing softmax logits produces embeddings that are too small, and too small relative to what gets added next.

### Telling the model where it is

Self-attention is permutation-equivariant: shuffle the input tokens and the outputs shuffle with them, unchanged. So [[positional-encoding]] exists purely because [[self-attention]] ignores order, and it is added once, at the bottom of each stack — not at every layer.

[[eq-posenc|The encoding used]] is [[sinusoidal-positional-encoding|a bank of fixed sinusoids]]: each dimension is a sine or cosine of the position, with wavelengths in a geometric progression from $2\pi$ to $10000 \cdot 2\pi$. It has width $d_{model}$ so it can be summed with the embedding rather than concatenated, which is why the embedding's scale matters at all.

Two reasons are given, and the paper hedges both. The stated hypothesis is [[relative-position-linearity]]: for a fixed offset $k$, $PE_{pos+k}$ is a linear function of $PE_{pos}$, so attending by relative position should be easy to learn. The second is that fixed sinusoids "may allow" extrapolation to sequences longer than any seen in training — a conjecture the paper does not test. Against [[learned-positional-embedding|learned position vectors]], results are near-identical ([[table-3]], row E), so the choice rests on the untested extrapolation argument.

### What this sets up

The layer is now fully specified: attention, feed-forward, residuals, and one order signal at the bottom. The open question is why the attention half is there instead of recurrence or convolution — which [[layer-comparison-desiderata|three criteria]] and [[table-1]] take up next.

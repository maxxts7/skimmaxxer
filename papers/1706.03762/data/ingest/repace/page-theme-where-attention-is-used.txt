You have the attention function. Where does the model actually put it, and why does the same function need three different wirings to build a translator?

Section 3.2.3 gives the answer: [[multi-head-attention]] appears three times, and the only thing that changes between them is where the queries come from and where the keys and values come from. Nothing about [[eq-attention|the formula]] changes. [[fig-1]] shows all three in one picture — two towers, no recurrence, no convolution.

### Encoder: every position, every other position

[[self-attention]] is the case where queries, keys, and values are all read from the same place. In [[encoder-self-attention]], that place is the output of the previous layer of the [[encoder-stack]]. Each position builds its new representation as a weighted mix of all positions, itself included, in one step.

The paper's reason for this is in Section 4, not Section 3. It compares layer types on three axes and reports in [[table-1]] that a self-attention layer connects any two positions with a [[maximum-path-length|path of length 1]] and a constant number of sequential steps, while a [[recurrent-neural-network|recurrent layer]] needs $O(n)$ of both. Convolutions sit in between: [[convolutional-sequence-models|ConvS2S and ByteNet]] relate distant positions in a number of operations that grows with distance, so a single layer does not connect everything. Short paths are argued to make [[long-range-dependencies]] easier to learn. The cost is $O(n^2 \cdot d)$ per layer, cheaper than a recurrent layer's $O(n \cdot d^2)$ only when $n < d$ — true for word-piece sentences, not for long documents. The paper's own escape hatch for that case, [[restricted-self-attention]], is proposed and left to future work, not measured.

Two consequences follow immediately. Because a position mixes all others by position-independent weights, the layer has no idea what order the tokens are in — which is why [[positional-encoding]] exists at all, added once at the bottom of each tower. And because one weighted average blurs things, the paper runs the attention in parallel heads; [[fig-3]] and [[fig-5]] show different heads in one encoder layer picking out different words. The paper calls this a qualitative illustration and says the patterns only "seem" to track sentence structure — [[attention-interpretability]] is offered as a side benefit, with no measurement behind it.

### Decoder: the same layer, minus the future

The decoder needs self-attention for the same reason the encoder does, but it cannot have the full version. The model generates one token at a time and conditions each on what it already emitted, so a decoder position seeing a later position at training time would be reading the answer. [[masked-decoder-self-attention]] fixes this with [[causal-masking]]: set the scores for forbidden positions to $-\infty$ before the [[softmax]], so their weights come out at zero. That single line is what preserves [[auto-regressive-generation]]. Combined with output embeddings offset by one position, predictions at position $i$ depend only on outputs before $i$.

### Between the stacks

The third use is the one that makes it a transduction model rather than two separate encoders. In [[encoder-decoder-attention]], queries come from the previous [[decoder-stack|decoder]] layer and keys and values come from the encoder's output. Every decoder position can look at the entire input sentence. This is not masked — the input is fully available. The paper says plainly that this mimics the encoder-decoder attention of earlier seq2seq models; the [[queries-keys-values|query/key/value]] split is what lets one function serve both roles.

You should now be able to look at any attention [[sub-layer]] in [[fig-1]] and say where its Q, K, and V come from, and whether it is masked. Next comes what has to be bolted on because attention alone is not enough: position information and the per-position feed-forward network.

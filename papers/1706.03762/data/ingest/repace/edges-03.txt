attention-heads-ablation --supported-by--> table-3 | label: rows (A) sweep the head count

Rows (A) vary h with total computation held constant. Single-head attention is 0.9 BLEU below the best setting, and quality also falls off with too many heads. This is the whole evidence for h=8 being a middle choice rather than more-is-better.

--- EDGE id=e042 ---
num-heads-h --supported-by--> table-3 | label: where h = 8 comes from

The base model's eight heads are not derived from anything; rows (A) of Table 3 are the measurement that picks the value. The sweep is on the English-to-German development set only, so it is one task's answer.

--- EDGE id=e043 ---
key-size-ablation --supported-by--> table-3 | label: rows (B) shrink d_k

Rows (B) reduce the attention key dimension and quality drops. The paper reads this as a hint that the dot product may be too simple a compatibility function, which is an interpretation of the number, not something the table tests.

--- EDGE id=e044 ---
size-and-dropout-ablation --supported-by--> table-3 | label: rows (C) and (D) vary size and dropout

Rows (C) change model dimensions and rows (D) change the dropout rate. The paper summarizes them in one sentence: bigger models are better, and dropout is very helpful in avoiding over-fitting.

--- EDGE id=e045 ---
residual-dropout --supported-by--> table-3 | label: row (D) shows the cost of removing it

P_drop = 0.1 in the base model is justified by the (D) rows, where turning dropout off or raising it degrades development-set quality. The table varies a single rate, so it does not separate the residual dropout from any other place dropout is applied.

--- EDGE id=e046 ---
label-smoothing --supported-by--> table-3 | label: reports BLEU and perplexity together

Section 5.4 claims label smoothing of 0.1 hurts perplexity but improves accuracy and BLEU. Table 3 is the only place both quantities are reported per configuration, so it is where that trade-off would be visible. The paper's prose about Table 3 comments on dropout and not on smoothing, so the claim is stated more firmly than it is demonstrated.

--- EDGE id=e047 ---
learned-positional-embedding --supported-by--> table-3 | label: row (E) finds no difference

Row (E) swaps sinusoids for learned positional embeddings and gets nearly identical results. This undercuts any quality argument for the sinusoids: the paper keeps them on the untested hope that they extrapolate to longer sequences than seen in training.

--- EDGE id=e048 ---
constituency-parsing --supported-by--> table-4 | label: reports the WSJ Section 23 F1

The generalization claim is carried entirely by this table: a 4-layer Transformer trained on the ~40K-sentence WSJ treebank reaches 91.3 F1 with almost no task-specific tuning, beating the BerkeleyParser in the same small-data setting.

--- EDGE id=e049 ---
semi-supervised-parsing --supported-by--> table-4 | label: lists the 17M-sentence result

Table 4 separates WSJ-only from semi-supervised rows, so the reader can see what the extra ~17M automatically parsed sentences buy. It is one run per setting, with dropout, learning rate, and beam size tuned on Section 22.

--- EDGE id=e050 ---
recurrent-neural-network-grammar --supported-by--> table-4 | label: the one row the Transformer loses to

Table 4 also records the limit of the claim: RNNG scores higher, so the paper says its model beats all previously reported models with that one exception. Worth showing readers, because it is the qualification the abstract leaves out.

Figure 1 puts the whole model in one picture: an N-layer encoder tower, an N-layer decoder tower, and the arrows carrying encoder output into each decoder layer's second attention sub-layer. It is a schematic, so it fixes wiring, not sizes.

The left half of Figure 2 redraws Equation (1) as boxes in order: MatMul of queries and keys, Scale, optional Mask, SoftMax, MatMul with values. It shows where the mask sits, which the equation itself does not.

The right half of Figure 2 shows the projections into h heads, the parallel attention blocks, the concatenation, and the final linear map. It makes the parallelism of the heads visible in a way the equation's Concat notation does not.

--- EDGE id=e054 ---
long-range-dependencies --supported-by--> fig-3 | label: a distant dependency, drawn

Figure 3 shows many heads in encoder layer 5 attending from 'making' to a far-away word that completes 'making...more difficult'. This is one hand-picked word in one sentence, so it illustrates that long-distance attention happens; it does not measure how often.

--- EDGE id=e055 ---
attention-averaging-resolution-cost --supported-by--> fig-4 | label: two heads split one word's attention

For the word 'its', head 5 and head 6 in the same layer put almost all their weight on different words. A single head would have averaged those two into one blurred distribution, which is the paper's stated reason for using several. The figure is a qualitative example, and the paper only says the heads are 'apparently' doing anaphora resolution.

--- EDGE id=e056 ---
attention-interpretability --supported-by--> fig-5 | label: two heads with different patterns

Figure 5 shows two heads from encoder layer 5 producing visibly different attention patterns on the same sentence, which is the basis for the claim that heads learn distinct, sometimes syntax-related roles. These are selected examples with no quantitative analysis, and the paper hedges with 'seems related'.

--- EDGE id=e057 ---
scaled-dot-product-attention --supported-by--> eq-attention | label: states the mechanism in one line

Equation (1) is the definition, not an illustration of it: softmax(QK^T/sqrt(d_k))V. Everything else in Section 3.2 refers back to this line, including the masked and encoder-decoder uses.

--- EDGE id=e058 ---
sqrt-dk-scaling --supported-by--> eq-attention | label: the divisor inside the softmax

The 1/sqrt(d_k) term appears in Equation (1) itself, and the footnote argues for it: with unit-variance components, a dot product over d_k dimensions has variance d_k. The paper says it 'suspects' large dot products flatten the softmax gradient and runs no ablation on the scaling factor.

--- EDGE id=e059 ---
multi-head-attention --supported-by--> eq-multihead | label: defines heads, concat, and W^O

The multi-head equation spells out that each head runs Equation (1) on its own projected queries, keys, and values, and that the outputs are concatenated and mapped back through W^O. With d_k = d_v = d_model/h = 64, it is also the reason the paper can say total cost is close to single-head attention at full width.

--- EDGE id=e060 ---
position-wise-feed-forward-network --supported-by--> eq-ffn | label: defines the second sub-layer

Equation (2), max(0, xW1 + b1)W2 + b2, is the whole feed-forward sub-layer. It shows the network sees one position at a time: there is no mixing across positions here, so all cross-position work happens in the attention sub-layers.

The paper's opening move is a complaint, not a proposal. Before it describes anything it builds, it has to establish that the dominant approach to [[sequence-transduction]] has a defect that no amount of tuning removes.

### The task and the incumbent

Sequence transduction is mapping one symbol sequence to another; the paper's benchmark is [[machine-translation|translation]]. The incumbent was the [[recurrent-neural-network|RNN]], in its gated forms — [[lstm]] and [[gated-recurrent-unit|GRU]] — which the paper describes as "firmly established as state of the art." It does not argue these models are weak. It argues they are shaped wrong for the hardware.

### The defect is structural

A recurrent model factors computation along positions: hidden state $h_t$ is a function of $h_{t-1}$ and the input at $t$. Two things follow. First, the [[sequential-computation-bottleneck]] — positions inside one training example cannot be computed in parallel. The paper adds the detail that makes this bite: at long sequence lengths, memory limits batching across examples, so you cannot buy back the lost parallelism there either. Second, a signal travelling between two distant positions passes through $O(n)$ steps, and the paper claims shorter paths make [[long-range-dependencies]] easier to learn.

Read the way the paper treats [[factorization-tricks]] and [[conditional-computation]]. It credits both with real efficiency gains, then says the constraint "remains." That is the hinge of the whole theme: those methods make each sequential step cheaper without reducing the number of steps. If the problem is the count, the fix has to change the architecture, not the arithmetic.

### The escape routes already tried

[[convolutional-sequence-models]] — [[extended-neural-gpu]], [[bytenet]], [[convs2s]] — took the parallelism half of the problem and solved it: all positions computed at once. But a kernel of width $k < n$ does not connect every pair of positions. Covering them takes $O(n/k)$ stacked layers, or $O(\log_k n)$ with [[dilated-convolutions]]. So distance still costs operations — linearly for ConvS2S, logarithmically for ByteNet — and the path-length problem comes back in a milder form. The paper also notes convolutional layers cost more per layer than recurrent ones, by a factor of $k$, unless you use [[separable-convolutions]].

[[end-to-end-memory-networks]] took the other half, building on recurrent attention rather than sequence-aligned recurrence. The paper cites them as evidence attention can carry a model, but on simple question answering and language modeling, not on transduction.

Each prior attempt trades one property for another. None gets parallel positions and short paths together.

### What the theme sets up

[[table-1]] is where this becomes numbers. Under the three criteria in [[layer-comparison-desiderata]], [[self-attention]] connects every position to every other in a constant number of sequential steps with a path of length 1, against $O(n)$ for both in a recurrent layer, and against stacked layers in a convolutional one. Its per-layer cost, $O(n^2 \cdot d)$, beats a recurrent layer's $O(n \cdot d^2)$ only when $n < d$ — a conditional win, and the paper states the condition rather than hiding it. Note also that Table 1 is asymptotic analysis, not measurement; the empirical case arrives later, in the translation results and [[table-4]].

Two costs come with the trade, and both drive later design. Attention averages over weighted positions, blurring resolution ([[attention-averaging-resolution-cost]]), which is the paper's stated reason for [[multi-head-attention]]. And dropping recurrence drops order information — [[fig-1]] shows a model with no mechanism anywhere for knowing which token came first, so position has to be injected at the bottom of each stack.

From here you can read any Transformer choice as an answer to one question: what did recurrence do for us that we now have to do another way?

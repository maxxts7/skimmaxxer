ITEM: table-4 (table) - The parsing leaderboard

TAKEAWAY:
Trained on the ~40K-sentence WSJ treebank with almost no task-specific tuning, a 4-layer Transformer reaches 91.3 F1 on WSJ Section 23 — above every listed discriminative parser except Dyer et al.'s RNN Grammar (91.7) — and 92.7 F1 when the extra ~17M auto-parsed sentences are added, the best semi-supervised number in the table. It also beats the RNN sequence-to-sequence parser (Vinyals & Kaiser et al.) whose experimental setup it borrows, in both regimes.

WALKTHROUGH:
You are looking at a small leaderboard for [[constituency-parsing|English constituency parsing]] — predicting a sentence's syntactic tree of nested phrases. It is the paper's only non-translation experiment, meant to test whether [[transformer|the Transformer]] transfers to a different kind of [[sequence-transduction|sequence transduction]] task, one where the output is longer than the input and must obey hard structural constraints.

### How to read it

Three columns. **Parser** names the system and its citation. **Training** names the data regime. **WSJ 23 F1** is the score, all rows measured the same way so the column is directly comparable.

*F1 here* is the parsing-evaluation F1 (EVALB): take every labelled constituent — a span of the sentence plus its label, e.g. `NP(3,7)` — in the predicted tree and in the gold tree. Precision is the fraction of predicted constituents that appear in the gold tree; recall is the fraction of gold constituents recovered. $F_1 = 2PR/(P+R)$, reported on a 0–100 scale. Section 23 of the [[penn-treebank-wsj|Penn Treebank WSJ]] is the standard held-out test split.

Horizontal rules group the rows by regime. The first block is *WSJ only, discriminative*: trained on ~40K hand-parsed sentences, scoring trees directly rather than modelling the sentence too. The second block is [[semi-supervised-parsing|semi-supervised]]: the same treebank plus roughly 17M automatically parsed sentences. The last two rows sit outside both — one multi-task, one generative — so they are context, not like-for-like comparisons.

### What it shows

The Transformer rows are the ones to anchor on: 91.3 WSJ-only and 92.7 semi-supervised. In the WSJ-only block that puts it above [[berkeley-parser|the BerkeleyParser]] (Petrov et al., 90.4) and Zhu et al. (90.4), and below [[recurrent-neural-network-grammar|the Recurrent Neural Network Grammar]] (Dyer et al., 91.7) — the one exception the paper names. In the semi-supervised block 92.7 is the top number.

Vinyals & Kaiser et al. appears in both blocks: it is the [[recurrent-neural-network|RNN]] sequence-to-sequence parser the Transformer copies its setup from. The Transformer beats it by 3.0 F1 WSJ-only and 0.6 semi-supervised. The paper stresses that only dropout rates, learning rate and [[beam-search|beam]] size were tuned (on the Section 22 dev set); everything else was left at the English-German base translation settings. So the claim is generalization, not a tuned parsing result.

NAME: Why recurrence had to go

THEME SUMMARY (shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat verbatim):
Sets up the task the paper cares about — turning one sequence into another — and the models that dominated it. Recurrent models process a sequence one position at a time, which the paper says blocks parallelism within a training example and stretches the path between distant positions. Convolutional models and attention-based memory networks are the prior attempts to get around this. This comes first because everything the paper proposes is a reaction to it.

EDGES BETWEEN MEMBERS OF THIS THEME:
  [supporting] convolutional-sequence-models --contrasts-with--> recurrent-neural-network | parallel positions, longer paths: Convolutions were the earlier answer to the same problem: they compute all positions in parallel, unlike recurrence, but the number of operations relating two positions grows with the distance between them. The paper also notes convolutional layers are generally more expensive than recurrent ones, by a factor of the kernel width k.

EVIDENCE ITEMS RELATED TO THIS THEME (link as [[item-id]]):
  - fig-1 | The two-stack data path: The figure shows the entire model in one picture: two towers of identical layers, each layer built only from attention and a position-wise feed-forward network, every sub-layer wrapped in a residual connection plus layer normalization. There is no recurrence and no convolution anywhere, so order information has to be injected once, at the bottom of each tower.
  - table-1 | Layer types by cost and path length: The table is the paper's argument for replacing recurrence with self-attention: a self-attention layer connects every position to every other in a constant number of sequential steps and a path of length 1, while a recurrent layer needs $O(n)$ of both. Its per-layer arithmetic cost, $O(n^2 \cdot d)$, is lower than a recurrent layer's $O(n \cdot d^2)$ whenever the sequence is shorter than the model is wide.
  - table-4 | The parsing leaderboard: Trained on the ~40K-sentence WSJ treebank with almost no task-specific tuning, a 4-layer Transformer reaches 91.3 F1 on WSJ Section 23 — above every listed discriminative parser except Dyer et al.'s RNN Grammar (91.7) — and 92.7 F1 when the extra ~17M auto-parsed sentences are added, the best semi-supervised number in the table. It also beats the RNN sequence-to-sequence parser (Vinyals & Kaiser et al.) whose experimental setup it borrows, in both regimes.

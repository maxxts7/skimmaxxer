NAME: Where attention is used in the model

THEME SUMMARY (shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat verbatim):
The same attention function appears in three places. In the encoder, self-attention lets every position look at every other position in the layer below. In the decoder, the same thing runs with a mask so a position can only see itself and earlier positions, which keeps generation auto-regressive. In between, the decoder attends over the encoder's output, with queries from the decoder and keys and values from the encoder.

EVIDENCE ITEMS RELATED TO THIS THEME (link as [[item-id]]):
  - fig-1 | The two-stack data path: The figure shows the entire model in one picture: two towers of identical layers, each layer built only from attention and a position-wise feed-forward network, every sub-layer wrapped in a residual connection plus layer normalization. There is no recurrence and no convolution anywhere, so order information has to be injected once, at the bottom of each tower.
  - table-1 | Layer types by cost and path length: The table is the paper's argument for replacing recurrence with self-attention: a self-attention layer connects every position to every other in a constant number of sequential steps and a path of length 1, while a recurrent layer needs $O(n)$ of both. Its per-layer arithmetic cost, $O(n^2 \cdot d)$, is lower than a recurrent layer's $O(n \cdot d^2)$ whenever the sequence is shorter than the model is wide.
  - fig-3 | Heads tracking "making … more difficult": This is one query word's attention pattern in a single encoder self-attention layer, drawn as lines from the word to the positions it attends to, colored by head. Several of the eight heads put most of their weight on "more" and "difficult", six and seven tokens away from "making" — the paper's illustration that self-attention can link distant, grammatically related words in a single step.
  - fig-5 | Two heads, one sentence: Two attention heads from the same encoder self-attention layer, run on the same sentence, produce visibly different attention patterns: one fans many positions into a few words, the other makes sparse, short, word-to-word links. The paper offers this as a qualitative illustration that heads specialize, and says only that the patterns "seem related" to sentence structure — nothing here is measured.

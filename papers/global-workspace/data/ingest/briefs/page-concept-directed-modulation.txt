NAME: Directed modulation

SUMMARY (already shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat it verbatim):
The property that the model can deliberately activate and compute with workspace vectors when instructed, independent of its outputs, and can pull normally absent information into the workspace when a task requires it.

DRAFT EXPLANATION from the extraction stage (your page replaces this; keep what is right, go deeper):
Same normalization: the five properties share [[workspace-like-properties|workspace-like-properties]] as parent.

THEME IT BELONGS TO: theme-five-workspace-properties | Five things the workspace does - This chapter takes the five properties of the paper's definition in turn. The model names concepts its workspace holds, loads the workspace on instruction, and carries the intermediate values of chained reasoning in it; one workspace vector also serves as an argument to many different downstream computations. Only a fraction of the model's behavior depends on the workspace at all, which the paper shows by suppressing it and recording which tasks survive and which collapse. It follows the instrument because each of these tests is a lens reading followed by an intervention on the same directions.

SUB-CONCEPTS (these render as expandable sections BELOW your page - reference them, do not duplicate them):
  - character-counting-task | Character-counting task: A task in which the model silently tracks the number of characters in each line of a passage, used to probe covert numeric content in the J-space.
  - hold-in-mind-copy-task | Hold-in-mind copying task: A protocol in which the model copies an unrelated sentence while an instruction tells it what to hold in mind; the lens is read at the copied tokens.
  - instruction-conditions | Instruction conditions (focus, ignore, mention, don't-think): The four instruction types compared in the modulation experiments, which produce different levels of target-concept activation in the J-space.
    - instruction-phrasing-variation | Phrasing spread within a condition: Each instruction condition is instantiated with five to eight different wordings, and the per-wording rates are plotted individually rather than only as a condition mean.
  - j-orthogonalized-property-probe | J-orthogonalized property probe: A linear probe for a stimulus property with its J-space component projected out, used to show that instructions move the J-space but not the underlying representation.
    - reverse-dissociation | Reverse dissociation (probe up, label absent): The second half of the dissociation: for some categories a real stimulus drives the property probe strongly while the property's name stays out of the J-lens entirely.
  - paired-question-protocol | Paired-question protocol: Presenting the same stimulus under two different questions to show that the question alone determines whether a property's label enters the J-space.
  - white-bear-effect | White bear effect: The human finding that instructions to suppress a thought increase its occurrence relative to no instruction at all; the paper observes a parallel in the J-space.
  - workspace-competition | Workspace competition under concurrent covert tasks: When the model holds two covert tasks at once, static concepts share workspace tokens freely, but an ongoing computation excludes other content from the tokens it occupies.
    - co-occupancy | Co-occupancy: A named quantity: at tokens where one covert task's content is in the lens top-10, the probability that the other task's content is too.
      - shuffled-occupancy-control | Shuffled partner control: The baseline against which co-occupancy is read: the partner task's presence at a token is taken from a different prompt, one where that task was given on its own.
    - dual-task-cost | Cost of holding two covert tasks: A separate measurement from token-level sharing: how often each task's content still reaches the top of the lens anywhere in the response when the two are given together rather than alone.
  - imagine-instruction-paradigm | Imagine-instruction paradigm: A three-way comparison in which a stimulus with one property is presented neutrally, presented under an instruction to imagine it has a different property, and set against a matched stimulus that genuinely has that other property.
  - modulation-trial-scoring | Scoring a modulation trial: The rule that decides whether a single modulation trial counts as a hit, which differs between the two concept-style task families and the line-width task.
  - no-instruction-baseline | No-instruction baseline: A control condition in which the target concept is never mentioned in the prompt, whose rate is approximately zero across all task families.
  - modulation-model-size-trend | Modulation and model size: Under a focus instruction, the rate at which the target reaches the J-lens tends to rise with model size; the paper states this as a tendency rather than a measured scaling law.

PREREQUISITES:
  - global-workspace-theory | Global workspace theory: A neuroscience theory holding that information becomes consciously accessible when it is posted to a shared, capacity-limited workspace that broadcasts it to many specialized brain processes.
  - j-space | J-space: The set of activation-space points expressible as a sparse nonnegative combination of J-lens vectors — the region of representation space the paper argues functions as a global workspace.
  - jacobian-lens | Jacobian lens (J-lens): The paper's new interpretability technique: for each vocabulary token, it identifies the vector direction that encodes the model's potential to verbalize that token, by averaging the linearized effect of activations on the token's likelihood over a large corpus.
  - workspace-like-properties | The five workspace-like properties: The paper's operational definition: a set of vector representations counts as workspace-like if it supports verbal report, directed modulation, internal reasoning, flexible generalization, and selectivity.

EVIDENCE ITEMS THAT SHOW THIS (link them as [[item-id]]):
  - fig-1 | The Five Workspace Tests: The figure states the paper's operational definition of a global workspace in a language model: a privileged subset of vector representations counts as workspace-like if it passes five functional tests — verbal report, directed modulation, internal reasoning, flexible generalization, and selectivity. Each test is paired with a stylized picture of a concrete experiment, run in full later in the paper.
  - fig-9 | Instructed Thoughts Under the Lens: Three interactive examples show that when the model is told to hold something in mind — a concept, a mental calculation, a silent count — that content rises to the top of J-lens readouts at output tokens whose visible text is unrelated, while none of it appears in what the model actually writes. This is the paper's qualitative demonstration that workspace contents respond to instruction alone.
  - fig-10 | Modulation scaling curves: Told to think about a concept while producing unrelated text, the model puts that concept at the top of the J-lens readout on most trials, and the rate tends to rise from Haiku to Sonnet to Opus. Told to ignore the concept, the model keeps the rate well below the focus level but not at zero, while the no-instruction baseline sits at approximately zero.
  - fig-64 | Oracle-lens phrase readouts on nine prompts: The oracle lens decodes a single activation into ten free-form phrases rather than a ranked list of single tokens, and across nine prompts those phrases name latent content that the surface text does not contain: that a stated drug dose is toxic, that an email is an attempt at blackmail, that a loop will raise a TypeError. It is a demonstration on selected examples, not a benchmark.
  - fig-65 | Phrasing spread of the four instruction conditions: Across three task families and three models, a bare mention of the target puts it in the J-space almost as often as an explicit focus instruction, and forbidding the thought suppresses it barely at all; only the ignore framing, which calls the concept task-irrelevant, pushes the rate well down, and even then not to the no-instruction floor. Within every condition the individual phrasings spread widely, so the control the paper reports is real but phrasing-sensitive.
  - fig-66 | Four more properties the question summons: Across four further text properties - British spelling, casual register, plural grammatical number and melancholy tone - the same passage read after a next-word question and after a name-the-property question loads different content into the workspace at identical tokens. In every panel the property's name reaches the J-lens top-10 at more stimulus positions under the naming question, while the model's next-word answer respects the property under both questions.
  - fig-67 | Imagining Python versus reading Python: An instruction to imagine that a JavaScript snippet is Python pushes the token python high in the J-lens readout at the snippet's own tokens, while a probe for Python-ness with its J-space part removed stays at its no-instruction baseline. Real Python code moves that probe by three to six standard deviations, and only sometimes moves the lens. The paper reads the pair of results as evidence that the instruction writes to the workspace rather than re-encoding the stimulus.
  - fig-73 | Two covert tasks competing for the same tokens: When the model is told to hold two things in mind while copying an unrelated sentence, both reach the workspace, but they share it differently: two concepts occupy the same tokens at about the rate a shuffled control predicts, while a concept and an ongoing arithmetic computation almost never occupy the same token. Splitting the workspace this way costs each task little overall reach, with the arithmetic answer paying the largest price.

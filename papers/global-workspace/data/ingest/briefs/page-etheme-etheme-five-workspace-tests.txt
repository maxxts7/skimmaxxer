NAME: The five tests a workspace has to pass

THEME SUMMARY (shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat verbatim):
Five properties state what would count as a workspace in a language model, and each is given its own test (fig-1). Asked to think of an item and then name it, the model names the concept its lens readout already carried, and exchanging that concept for another in lens coordinates changes the answer it gives (fig-6). Some agreement between readout and speech is guaranteed by the way the lens is built, so the swapped-in items are drawn from outside the model's own top 10 and the weight is put on the causal effect rather than the agreement. Instructions move what the workspace holds without moving the property itself: a probe for the property, with its J-space component projected out, stays where it was while the property's name enters the readout. Swapping an unspoken intermediate changes the final answer (fig-13), one identical edit redirects sixteen different function templates (fig-19), and a lens-free probe for the same intermediate reaches the same conclusion when only its small J-space part is exchanged (fig-16).

MEMBER EDGES (19) - these render as a list BELOW your page. Your page is the argument they add up to, not a re-listing:
  e026 [supporting] swap-effect-magnitude (Magnitude of swap effect) --depends-on--> workspace-loading (Workspace loading) | graded effect plotted against loading: Beyond pass or fail, the paper measures how far a swap shifts the output toward the target's answer. That quantity is plotted against workspace loading, the cosine similarity between the residual stream and the concept's lens vector on an unmodified pass. The graded result therefore requires a loading measurement taken before any intervention is applied.
  e030 [supporting] out-of-top-10-swap-targets (Swap targets drawn from outside the top 10) --depends-on--> report-relation-by-construction (Report relation expected by construction) | design guards a built-in agreement: The J-lens is built from the causal effect of activations on output tokens, so some agreement between its readouts and what the model says is guaranteed by construction. The report experiments therefore draw the swapped-in item from the same category but exclude anything already in the top 10 of the model's own output distribution. The design choice is forced by the method's own circularity.
  e031 [load-bearing] j-space-privilege-for-report (Privilege of the J-space component for report) --depends-on--> j-space-component-decomposition (J-space component decomposition) | privilege test needs the split: To show that the J-space part of a concept's representation is what supports report, the representation first has to be cut into a J-space component and a non-J-space remainder. The two pieces are then injected at equal norm. The paper's finding that the small J-space piece carries the reportability depends entirely on that decomposition being available.
  e032 [supporting] j-orthogonalized-property-probe (J-orthogonalized property probe) --depends-on--> linear-probe (Linear probe) | probe built then orthogonalized: The dissociation argument needs a reading of a stimulus property that does not come from the lens. The paper trains a supervised linear probe for the property and then projects out its J-space component. The result — that instructions move the J-space while the underlying property stays put — depends on having a J-lens-independent probe to compare against.
  e033 [load-bearing] workspace-like-properties (The five workspace-like properties) --supported-by--> fig-1 (The Five Workspace Tests (fig-1)) | defines what the tests must show: Figure 1 measures nothing. It sets out the five tests a set of representations must pass to count as workspace-like: verbal report, directed modulation, internal reasoning, flexible generalization and selectivity. Every experiment in section 3 is scored against that list, so the figure fixes in advance what would count as a failure.
  e039 [load-bearing] verbal-report (Verbal report from the J-space) --supported-by--> fig-6 (The Soccer-to-Rugby Swap (fig-6)) | report follows workspace contents: At the colon just before the model names the sport it thought of, Soccer already sits high in the lens readout, and exchanging the Soccer coordinate for Rugby makes the model answer Rugby. Across fourteen categories the lens ordering of ten candidate answers tracks the next-token logit ordering, and swaps move targets that began below rank 11 to the top of the output.
  e040 [load-bearing] j-space-privilege-for-report (Privilege of the J-space component for report) --supported-by--> fig-8 (The matched-norm component test (fig-8)) | small component carries the report: A concept vector is split into its J-space component and the remainder. The J-space component holds a median of only 6 to 7 percent of the vector's variance, yet swapping along it reaches the top five on 59 percent of trials against 88 percent for the pure lens vectors, while the remainder, carrying roughly 93 percent of the variance, succeeds on 5 percent.
  e041 [load-bearing] directed-modulation (Directed modulation) --supported-by--> fig-10 (Modulation scaling curves (fig-10)) | instruction rates against a null baseline: The no-instruction baseline is approximately zero across all three task families, so the prompt context alone does not put the target in the readout. Under a focus instruction the target reaches the top of the lens on a substantial fraction of trials, and the rise with model size is reported as a tendency rather than a measured scaling law.
  e042 [load-bearing] internal-reasoning (Internal reasoning) --supported-by--> fig-13 (Swapping the unspoken step (fig-13)) | swapping the unspoken step: Each row shows a prompt whose answer depends on a concept the model never writes. Exchanging spider for ant turns the answer from 8 to 6, and exchanging the planned rhyme fight for light changes word choices earlier in the line, from "coming fight" to "morning light". The figure is a handful of hand-picked prompts; the systematic version follows in Figure 15.
  e043 [supporting] swap-depth-control (Intermediate-versus-answer swap depth control) --supported-by--> fig-15 (Swap success and depth control (fig-15)) | rules out a smuggled answer: Swapping the intermediate flips the top-1 output on 54 percent of trials for Haiku 4.5 and 70 percent for both Sonnet 4.5 and Opus 4.5. The depth control answers the obvious objection: if the intermediate's vector merely carried the answer, both swaps would bite at the same depth, and instead the intermediate swap takes effect a median of about 17 percent earlier in the network.
  e044 [load-bearing] intermediate-probe (Intermediate probe) --supported-by--> fig-16 (The split-probe swap test (fig-16)) | lens-free probe reaches the same conclusion: The intermediate is represented here without the J-lens, by averaging activations over prompts that imply it through different surface cues, which removes the circularity of testing the lens with the lens. Across 90 prompts, swapping the probe's J-space component flips the answer on 61 percent of trials against 60 percent for the raw lens vectors, while the non-J-space remainder manages 28 percent and falls to 6 percent once the J-space coordinates are clamped.
  e045 [load-bearing] flexible-generalization (Flexible generalization (broadcast)) --supported-by--> fig-19 (Swap Success Across Sixteen Templates (fig-19)) | one swap across sixteen functions: The same edit, applied unchanged in every prompt, redirects the model's top answer on 76 of 192 trials, and on 101 of 192 when the swap is applied at double strength. The broadcast claim rests on a success rate under half at the default strength, and the paper attributes the failures to low workspace loading rather than to absent broadcast, an explanation it cannot separate from the alternative that number words are simply misaligned with their lens vectors.
  e084 [minor] thought-suppression (Thought-suppression experiments) --instance-of--> white-bear-effect (White bear effect) | the white bear paradigm, in a model: The human finding is that an instruction not to think of something raises its occurrence above a no-instruction baseline. The paper's suppression experiments are the model-side run of that paradigm, and both the base and post-trained models fail to keep the forbidden concept out of the workspace.
  e091 [supporting] fig-19 (Swap Success Across Sixteen Templates (fig-19)) --instance-of--> swap-success-chart (Swap-success chart) | a swap scoreboard: The shape is a pass-or-fail scoreboard for the lens-coordinate swap. Figure 19 fills it with sixteen function templates: one identical swap redirects the model's top answer on 76 of 192 trials.
  e109 [load-bearing] anomaly-detection-condition (Anomaly detection under a swapped language) --contrasts-with--> verbal-report (Verbal report from the J-space) | same swap moves report only: The same Spanish-to-French lens swap is applied across the question tokens in four tasks over one passage. Explicit report and the flexible questions flip to the French answer on essentially every trial, while continuation and anomaly detection are unmoved. The word Spanish appears in the readouts at comparable rates in all four conditions, so presence in the workspace and causal role are separate things.
  e117 [supporting] null-swap-interpretation (Reading a swap that does nothing) --contrasts-with--> workspace-loading (Workspace loading) | no routing or nothing loaded: A swap that changes nothing is used as evidence that a computation does not route through the J-space. The paper records the competing reading: swap failures concentrate in cases where the source concept was only weakly present before any intervention. Country arguments have the highest workspace loading and swap most reliably, while number words have the lowest and swap worst.
  e122 [supporting] j-space-privilege-for-report (Privilege of the J-space component for report) --contrasts-with--> non-j-space-residual-effect (The residual effect of the non-J-space component) | small component carries the report: A concept's full representation splits into a small piece inside the J-space and a large remainder outside it. Injected at equal norm, the small piece drives the model's report and the remainder does much less. The remainder is not inert, and the paper tests whether its residual effect is itself routed through the J-space by clamping the relevant lens coordinates at their clean-pass values.
  e123 [load-bearing] j-orthogonalized-property-probe (J-orthogonalized property probe) --contrasts-with--> directed-modulation (Directed modulation) | label moves, underlying property does not: An instruction to imagine that a JavaScript snippet is Python raises python in the lens by several standard deviations while leaving a Python probe with its J-space component projected out at baseline; a real Python snippet moves that probe by three to six standard deviations. The dissociation also runs the other way, with a past-tense sentence driving the tense probe six standard deviations while past stays far down the readout. Instructions write to the workspace rather than re-encoding the stimulus.
  e130 [load-bearing] report-relation-by-construction (Report relation expected by construction) --contrasts-with--> verbal-report (Verbal report from the J-space) | guaranteed agreement versus causal evidence: The J-lens is built from the average causal effect of activations on output tokens, so some agreement between its readouts and what the model says is guaranteed by construction. The verbal-report property therefore cannot rest on agreement alone. It rests on the swaps: exchanging one active workspace vector for another changes which item the model names.

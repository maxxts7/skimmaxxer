NAME: Lens-coordinate swap

SUMMARY (already shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat it verbatim):
An intervention that exchanges one concept for another: it reads an activation's coordinates along two J-lens vectors, permutes them, and writes the result back, leaving everything orthogonal to those vectors unchanged.

DRAFT EXPLANATION from the extraction stage (your page replaces this; keep what is right, go deeper):
Given a source token $s$ and target token $t$, the paper forms $V = [v_s\;v_t]$, reads the lens coordinates $c = V^\dagger h$ (where $V^\dagger$ is the pseudoinverse of $V$), and sets $h_{\text{patched}} = h + V(\sigma(c) - c)$, where $\sigma$ swaps the two entries of $c$, optionally scaled by a factor $\alpha$. The component of $h$ orthogonal to the span of the two vectors is unchanged, so the intervention exchanges exactly one concept for another while holding the rest of the activation fixed. Swaps are the paper's sharpest causal test: if the model's answer flips to the one implied by the swapped-in concept, the swapped direction was carrying that concept's role in the computation. Swaps are used to validate lens vectors against alternatives, to test [[attribution-graphs]], and — with template vectors — to swap multi-token concepts.

THEME IT BELONGS TO: theme-the-jacobian-lens | The Jacobian lens - The paper's instrument is one matrix per layer, built by averaging the Jacobian from that layer to the model's output over a corpus of prompts. It yields one vector per token in the vocabulary: the direction that, on average, disposes the model to say that token. This chapter covers how the matrix is built, how a readout is taken from it, and how a concept is steered, ablated or swapped along its vectors, together with the earlier tools the lens is measured against — the logit lens, the tuned lens, linear probes and sparse dictionaries. The instrument comes before the findings because every finding is a lens measurement.

  - implied-counterfactual-answer | The target-appropriate answer: A swap is scored against the answer the prompt would have had if the swapped-in concept were the true one, not against the swapped-in concept itself.
  - oblique-lens-coordinates | Coordinates in a non-orthogonal frame: J-lens vectors are neither orthogonal nor unit-norm, so the swap solves for the two coefficients with a pseudoinverse rather than taking plain inner products.
  - null-swap-interpretation | Reading a swap that does nothing: A swap with no effect is used as evidence that a computation does not route through the J-space, though the paper also records that a failed swap can mean the concept was never strongly present.
  - cross-intermediate-swap | Swapping the same value into different slots: The same replacement value is written into two different intermediate nodes and produces two different answers, which the paper takes as showing each intermediate is operated on by the computation that follows it.
  - swap-trial-grid | The per-trial swap grid: Every swap trial of the flexible-generalization experiment is laid out as one 4x4 grid per function template, with rows the argument present in the prompt and columns the argument swapped in.

EVIDENCE ITEMS THAT SHOW THIS (link them as [[item-id]]):
  - fig-1 | The Five Workspace Tests: The figure states the paper's operational definition of a global workspace in a language model: a privileged subset of vector representations counts as workspace-like if it passes five functional tests — verbal report, directed modulation, internal reasoning, flexible generalization, and selectivity. Each test is paired with a stylized picture of a concrete experiment, run in full later in the paper.
  - fig-4 | The Jacobian lens, end to end: One schematic covers the paper's whole instrument: how the per-layer matrix $J_\ell$ is built by averaging backpropagated Jacobians over positions and a thousand prompts, how it turns any mid-network activation into a ranked vocabulary list, and how the same machinery supports a surgical concept swap that leaves the rest of the activation untouched.
  - fig-6 | The Soccer-to-Rugby Swap: On a prompt asking the model to think of an item and then name it, the J-lens readout at the position just before the answer already contains the answer, and exchanging that lens content — Soccer out, Rugby in — makes the model report the swapped concept instead. The verbal report follows the contents of the workspace at the time of reporting, causally and not just correlationally.
  - fig-8 | The matched-norm component test: A concept's full representation is split into a small piece inside the J-space and a large remainder outside it, and the two pieces are injected at equal magnitude. Only the J-space piece moves the model's verbal reports: 59% swap success against 5% for the remainder, next to 88% for pure J-lens vectors. The paper takes this as evidence that the J-space is privileged for report, not merely correlated with it.
  - fig-13 | Swapping the unspoken step: Exchanging an unspoken intermediate concept for an alternative, purely in J-lens coordinates, changes the model's final answer to the one the swapped-in concept implies. Across three worked examples — a two-hop fact, a planned rhyme, and a cross-language antonym — the output tracks the substituted intermediate, which the paper takes as evidence that these lens vectors are causally load-bearing for internal reasoning, not just readable.
  - fig-14 | Bandit Plan Swap: In a two-option reward task, the workspace carries the model's chosen strategy — repeat or switch — rather than an echo of the prompt, which names both. Swapping the two conditions' strategy directions flips the model's A/B choice in both directions, so the strategy content is causally load-bearing.
  - fig-15 | Swap success and depth control: Exchanging an unspoken intermediate concept for an alternative in J-lens coordinates moves the alternative's implied answer to the model's top-1 output on 54% of two-hop prompts for Haiku 4.5 and 70% for both Sonnet 4.5 and Opus 4.5. A depth comparison shows the intermediate swap takes effect a median of about 17% of depth earlier than a swap of the answer itself, which the paper takes as evidence that the intermediate is used before the answer is computed, not that its lens vector smuggles the answer in.
  - fig-16 | The split-probe swap test: A representation of an unspoken intermediate, extracted without the J-lens, keeps most of its variance outside the J-space — yet swapping only its small J-space component flips the model's answer on 61% of 90 two-hop prompts, matching the raw J-lens swap at 60%, while swapping the much larger remainder succeeds on only 28%, and on 6% once the J-space coordinates are held fixed.
  - fig-18 | One Swap, Many Functions: A single, identical edit — exchanging the J-lens vector for France with that of another country at every token position — makes the model answer capital, language, and continent questions about the swapped-in country instead. One representation, changed once, redirects many different downstream computations, which is the broadcast behavior a global workspace predicts.
  - fig-19 | Swap Success Across Sixteen Templates: One J-lens vector, swapped identically into sixteen different function contexts, redirects the model's top answer on 76 of 192 trials, and on 101 of 192 when the swap is applied at double strength. Failures concentrate where the source concept was only weakly loaded in the workspace before any intervention, so workspace loading predicts whether the swap lands. Together the two panels are the paper's quantitative case that a single shared representation is read by many downstream computations.
  - fig-20 | Four Tasks Over One Spanish Passage: The passage's language, Spanish, appears in the J-lens readouts at similar rates during all four tasks, but swapping it to French changes the model's answer only under explicit report and flexible computation. Continuation and anomaly detection produce the same output either way, so the workspace representation is present but causally idle during automatic processing.
  - fig-21 | A character count pulled in on demand: The same passage is shown under three questions, and a running character count that the model always computes appears in its workspace only when a question asks for the count or needs it for a further step. Swapping the count inside the workspace changes the model's answer in exactly those two conditions, while the automatic line-wrapping decision that also depends on the count is untouched.
  - fig-54 | Swap success across three model scales: Exchanging one concept for another along J-lens directions flips the model's top-1 answer to the implied one far more often than the same exchange along logit-lens or tuned-lens directions, and the ordering holds on all three model sizes tested. This is a causal check rather than a readout check: it tests whether the lens found a direction the model actually computes with, not merely one that displays the right word.
  - fig-61 | Template lens versus J-lens on multi-token words: Three side-by-side comparisons show that the template lens names whole multi-token words — blackmail, photosynthesis, Tchaikovsky — where the J-lens returns only word-initial fragments, and that swapping one template for another at the workspace layers flips the model's answer in a way the corresponding fragment vectors cannot. The panels are hand-picked examples; the paper treats the template lens as a proof of concept rather than a finished method.
  - fig-62 | Lens accuracy versus word length: Across multi-hop prompts whose unspoken intermediate concept is one to four-plus tokens long, the J-lens decodes and swaps single-token concepts well and then falls off sharply as the concept grows longer, while the template lens holds roughly flat at every length. On single-token concepts the two are comparable, with the template lens slightly behind on readout and ahead on swaps.
  - fig-63 | Template lens versus J-lens on single-token intermediates: Re-running the appendix's readout and swap evaluations on the paper's original 50-item multi-hop benchmark, where every unspoken intermediate is a single token, leaves the two lenses at rough parity: the template lens reads out slightly more intermediates (84% against 76%) and redirects slightly fewer answers (52% against 68%). Both gaps sit inside overlapping 95% Wilson intervals, so the figure supports only the claim that the template lens buys nothing here, not that either lens is better.
  - fig-68 | Per-Trial Swap Grids: This is the trial-by-trial record behind the flexible-generalization headline count: sixteen small grids, one per function template, showing what the model actually said after every individual lens-coordinate swap. It exposes a sharp gradient the summary number hides - country swaps redirect the answer almost perfectly at 42 of 48 off-diagonal cells, while number relations succeed on none of 48 at standard strength. The grey ranks in the failing cells show the target answer usually sits somewhere below the top rather than absent, which is the paper's evidence that weak swaps push in the right direction without going far enough.
  - fig-89 | Attribution graph for a two-step calculation: A J-lens attribution graph of the prompt "(4+17)*2+7=" names the three intermediates 21, 42 and 49 as nodes in computation order, and shows the multiplication entering as a fused "doubled" operation rather than as an operator plus a literal 2. Coordinate swaps confirm the named intermediates are causally load-bearing, while the first addition's operands are not, which the paper reads as that step happening outside the J-space.
  - eq-lens-swap | Swapping two concepts in lens coordinates: These three expressions define the paper's concept-exchange surgery: read how much of a source concept and a target concept an activation carries along their two J-lens directions, exchange those two numbers, and write the difference back. Everything the activation carries outside the plane spanned by the two lens vectors is left exactly as it was, so the edit swaps one concept for another rather than replacing the activation.

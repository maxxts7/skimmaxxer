NAME: J-space

SUMMARY (already shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat it verbatim):
The set of activation-space points expressible as a sparse nonnegative combination of J-lens vectors — the region of representation space the paper argues functions as a global workspace.

DRAFT EXPLANATION from the extraction stage (your page replaces this; keep what is right, go deeper):
At each layer, the J-lens vectors form an overcomplete set: $n_{\text{vocab}}$ vectors in $d_{\text{model}}$-dimensional space, so they may span the entire [[residual-stream]] and admit many decompositions of any activation. Empirically, however, only a relatively small number of J-lens vectors are strongly active at a time, so the paper defines the J-space as the points expressible as a sparse nonnegative combination of J-lens vectors, for some sparsity level $k$ — a somewhat arbitrary parameter, typically chosen to be no more than 25, the number of vectors observed to be meaningfully active at once. Geometrically, the J-space is a union of $k$-dimensional cones, one per possible set of $k$ J-lens vectors. Under the superposition hypothesis, the J-lens vectors would constitute a token-indexed subframe of the model's feature directions, with the features outside this subframe making up the bulk of the model's representations. The J-space component of activations is small — never more than 10% of activation variance — yet the paper's central claim is that this small component behaves like a global workspace.

THEME IT BELONGS TO: theme-shape-of-the-workspace | Where the workspace sits and what it holds - Where the previous chapter asks what the workspace does, this one asks where in the model it sits and how much it can hold. The J-space carries workspace-like content only in a middle band of layers: it is nearly empty for roughly the first third of depth, and gives way to output-bound representations near the end. Sparse reconstruction puts its occupancy at tens of concepts per position and under a tenth of activation variance, a limit the paper compares with human working memory. The model's weights amplify and relay J-space directions more than comparable directions, which the paper reads as broadcast, and the commitment to one reading of an ambiguous input sharpens across the same band.

SUB-CONCEPTS (these render as expandable sections BELOW your page - reference them, do not duplicate them):
  - j-space-broadcast | Broadcast privilege of J-lens vectors: The structural claim that the model's weights are configured to distribute J-space content disproportionately widely — via MLPs across depth and attention heads across token positions.
    - broadcast-heads | Broadcast heads: A distinctive top-1% subset of workspace-layer attention heads that selectively relays J-space content between token positions, identified by OV gain and label preservation.
      - broadcast-head-ablation | Broadcast-head ablation: Zeroing the broadcast heads' outputs disrupts J-space contents (recall@25 falls to 0.67 vs 0.86 for random heads) and relay-dependent behaviors, while barely changing next-token output.
      - label-preservation | Label preservation: A metric for whether a head copies directions faithfully rather than scrambling them: the mean reciprocal rank of $\cos(W_{OV}v_i, v_i)$ among all $\cos(W_{OV}v_i, v_j)$.
      - broadcast-head-selection-rule | Broadcast-head selection rule: How the two head metrics are combined: rank all workspace-layer heads on gain and on label preservation, score each head by its worse rank across the two, and take the best 1%.
      - broadcast-head-layer-placement | Where the broadcast heads sit: The heads selected for the J-lens vectors concentrate in the first half of the workspace layers, where the J-space's effective rank is lowest.
    - composition-score | Composition score: A weights-only measure of how unevenly a direction projects across attention heads' weight matrices; high scores against value weights mean some heads are specifically oriented to carry the direction.
      - value-vs-query-key-asymmetry | Value-output versus query-key asymmetry: J-space directions compose strongly with attention value and output weights but only weakly with query and key weights, which the paper reads as content being moved in J-space format while routing is less J-space-aligned.
    - mlp-gain | MLP gain: How strongly the next MLP block amplifies information along a unit direction: output norm on that direction, normalized by the median over random directions.
      - mlp-gain-context-caveat | Context dependence of MLP gain: The gain measurement applies the MLP block to a direction on its own, which is never how the block is used in practice; the paper flags it as a first-pass approximation.
    - neuron-connectivity | Neuron connectivity analyses: Weight-level corroboration of depth broadcast: MLP neurons' strongest weight alignments fall disproportionately on J-space-aligned directions, in breadth and in strength.
      - tail-energy | Tail energy ratio: A connection-strength measure: the sum of squared neuron-weight cosines above a random-chance threshold, normalized so random directions score 1.
      - neuron-top-match-breadth | Top-match breadth analysis: The breadth half of the connectivity analysis: each MLP neuron is assigned to whichever population its single strongest weight cosine falls in, and J-aligned populations claim far more neurons than chance.
      - read-write-asymmetry | Read-side and write-side asymmetry: J-space alignment of MLP neuron input weights peaks in the early workspace layers, while alignment of output weights peaks in the late ones.
    - broadcast-control-populations | Control populations for broadcast measurements: The set of direction populations every broadcast metric is run on, so that J-lens vectors are always scored against comparably shaped alternatives rather than in isolation.
      - j-rotated-control | J-rotated control population: A control made by applying one fixed random orthogonal rotation to the J-lens vectors, keeping their spectrum and pairwise geometry while destroying their alignment with the model's weights.
      - kappa-strata | SAE kurtosis strata: SAE decoder directions split into six equal-sized bins by J-lens kurtosis percentile, giving a graded rather than binary measure of how J-space-aligned a feature is.
    - random-direction-normalization | Normalization against random directions: Every broadcast metric is divided by the same quantity computed on isotropic random unit directions, so a score of 1 means no more than chance.
  - j-space-capacity | J-space capacity: The finding that the J-space is limited: it holds on the order of tens of concepts at a time, carries under 10% of activation variance, and excludes most of the model's representational features.
    - excess-variance-explained | Excess variance explained: The fraction of activation variance the top-$K$ J-lens vectors explain beyond a same-size random control — modest, never exceeding 10%.
    - j-space-occupancy | J-space occupancy: The number of J-lens vectors meaningfully active in an activation: the value of $K$ at which sparse decomposition stops beating a random control, plateauing around 25 in the workspace band.
    - single-layer-capacity | Capacity at a single layer: Repeating the list analysis at one workspace layer (L79): qualitatively the same, but with only one or two unrelated concepts encoded at a time instead of six.
    - word-list-experiment | Word-list loading experiment: A paradigm for measuring functional capacity: the model reads 80-word comma-separated lists while the J-lens counts which list words are present in the top-25 readout at each comma.
      - category-displacement | Category displacement: The finding that a category switch, not the passage of tokens, clears the J-space: after even one word of a new category, the old category's words are largely evicted.
      - category-loading | Category loading: The finding that related words load the J-space as a category: nearly a whole word family becomes present after a few items, including words not yet read.
      - unrelated-list-capacity | Capacity for unrelated words: With lists of unrelated words, only about six of the words read so far are present in the readout at any comma, and the count stays flat as the list grows.
      - band-min-rank | Best rank over the workspace band: The pooling rule used to score presence: a word's rank is its best rank across the workspace layers L38-92, so a word counts as present if any one of those layers carries it.
      - rank-threshold-robustness | Sensitivity to the top-25 cutoff: A check that the list findings do not depend on the rank cutoff chosen: the count of already-read words is plotted as the threshold $K$ varies, against control words that never appear in the prompt.
    - vectors-versus-concepts | Vectors counted, not concepts: The caveat that occupancy counts active J-lens vectors, which is not the same as counting ideas, because several vectors can together stand for one broader concept.
    - per-position-capacity | A limit per token position: The capacity limit applies to what the J-space holds at one token position; attention can still retrieve representations from any earlier position, so the limit does not bound what the model has access to.
  - j-space-component | J-space and non-J-space components: For any activation, its J-space component is the nearest point in the J-space; the non-J-space component is the leftover difference.
  - j-space-component-decomposition | J-space component decomposition: Splitting a concept's representation into a small J-space component and a large non-J-space remainder, to test which part carries causal effects.
    - concept-vector | Concept vector: A model's full representation of a concept, extracted as a mean residual-stream activation on prompts about that concept.
    - gradient-pursuit | Gradient pursuit: The greedy sparse-approximation algorithm the paper uses to find a sparse nonnegative combination of J-lens vectors reconstructing an activation.
    - non-j-space-residual-effect | The residual effect of the non-J-space component: The non-J-space remainder of a concept's representation still moves behavior a little, and the paper tests whether that residual effect is itself routed through the J-space.
  - j-space-formalization | Formalization of the J-space: The paper's formal account of what kind of mathematical object the J-space is — a sparse subframe defining a union of cones — together with distance and containment notions for comparing workspace candidates.
    - j-space-distance | Distances between workspace candidates: The distance function $d_\mathcal{F}(x)$ from an activation to the nearest cone, the between-candidate distance $\Delta_\mu$, and the one-sided containment measure $D_\mu$.
    - sparse-subframe | Sparse subframe: The paper's name for the J-lens vectors coupled with a sparsity level $k$: a frame (not necessarily independent vectors), restricted to sparse combinations, that does not span the whole space under that restriction.
    - union-of-cones | Union of cones: The geometric form of the J-space: for sparsity $k$, the union over all size-$k$ subsets of J-lens vectors of the cones of their nonnegative combinations.
    - workspace-candidate | Workspace candidates: The paper treats the J-space as one constructible approximation to an underlying workspace the model contains, and the formalization exists so that competing constructions can be compared.
    - sparsity-relaxations | Proposed relaxations of the definition: Section 10.8 lists three ways its own definition could be generalized: approximating the distance function, replacing the fixed $k$ with an $\ell_1$ penalty, and restricting which combinations are allowed.
  - lens-kurtosis | Lens kurtosis (κ): A statistic operationalizing whether a direction lies in the J-space: project it through the J-lens and measure how sharply peaked the resulting vocabulary distribution is.
    - bookkeeping-features | Syntactic and bookkeeping features: The low-κ majority of SAE features: low-level syntactic and surface-level features whose lens readouts are unrelated to their apparent meaning.
    - motor-features | Motor features: High-κ SAE features that activate immediately prior to, and promote, the utterance of a specific token.
    - workspace-features | Workspace features: High-κ SAE features that name an abstract concept and activate when it is invoked in context, without the model being about to say it.
  - sparse-decomposition | Sparse decomposition: Solving for a sparse nonnegative combination of $k$ J-lens vectors that best reconstructs an activation, yielding a discrete inventory of active concepts and their coefficients.
    - local-j-space-coordinates | Local J-space coordinates: The coefficients an activation carries on the J-lens vectors that reconstruct it; local because the overcomplete frame admits no single global coordinate system.
  - sparse-frame | Sparse frame and subframe: The formal footing for the J-space: if activations decompose into sparsely active linear features, those features form a sparse frame spanning activation space, and the J-space is a sparse subframe of it.
  - per-position-j-space | The J-space is per layer and per token position: The J-space is defined separately at each layer and at each token position, so the model's workspace at a given moment is a family of per-position sets rather than one shared store.
  - sparsity-level-k | Choosing the sparsity level k: The number $k$ is fixed by hand for each experiment rather than derived, and the paper uses values between 10 and 25 for two different purposes.

PREREQUISITES:
  - global-workspace-theory | Global workspace theory: A neuroscience theory holding that information becomes consciously accessible when it is posted to a shared, capacity-limited workspace that broadcasts it to many specialized brain processes.
  - j-lens-vector | J-lens vector: One vector per vocabulary token per layer: the direction in residual-stream space that, on average across contexts, disposes the model to say that token.
  - jacobian-lens | Jacobian lens (J-lens): The paper's new interpretability technique: for each vocabulary token, it identifies the vector direction that encodes the model's potential to verbalize that token, by averaging the linearized effect of activations on the token's likelihood over a large corpus.
  - linear-representation-hypothesis | Linear concept representations: The working assumption that language models encode concepts as directions in activation space, with an activation decomposing into a sum of sparsely active linear features.
  - superposition | Superposition: The hypothesis that a model's activations decompose as sparse linear combinations drawn from an overcomplete set of feature directions — more features than dimensions.
  - workspace-layers | Workspace layer range: The finding that the J-space plays a workspace-like role only in a middle range of layers: nearly empty for roughly the first third of depth, and giving way in the final layers to representations of the imminent output.

EVIDENCE ITEMS THAT SHOW THIS (link them as [[item-id]]):
  - fig-2 | Three Structural Signatures: The J-space carries workspace-like content only in a middle band of layers, holds few concepts at a time there, and is wired for broadcast: the model's MLPs and a small set of attention heads distribute its contents far more widely than other directions. The figure is a stylized preview of these three structural claims, which section 4 then establishes by measurement.
  - fig-4 | The Jacobian lens, end to end: One schematic covers the paper's whole instrument: how the per-layer matrix $J_\ell$ is built by averaging backpropagated Jacobians over positions and a thousand prompts, how it turns any mid-network activation into a ranked vocabulary list, and how the same machinery supports a surgical concept swap that leaves the rest of the activation untouched.
  - fig-8 | The matched-norm component test: A concept's full representation is split into a small piece inside the J-space and a large remainder outside it, and the two pieces are injected at equal magnitude. Only the J-space piece moves the model's verbal reports: 59% swap success against 5% for the remainder, next to 88% for pure J-lens vectors. The paper takes this as evidence that the J-space is privileged for report, not merely correlated with it.
  - fig-9 | Instructed Thoughts Under the Lens: Three interactive examples show that when the model is told to hold something in mind — a concept, a mental calculation, a silent count — that content rises to the top of J-lens readouts at output tokens whose visible text is unrelated, while none of it appears in what the model actually writes. This is the paper's qualitative demonstration that workspace contents respond to instruction alone.
  - fig-20 | Four Tasks Over One Spanish Passage: The passage's language, Spanish, appears in the J-lens readouts at similar rates during all four tasks, but swapping it to French changes the model's answer only under explicit report and flexible computation. Continuation and anomaly detection produce the same output either way, so the workspace representation is present but causally idle during automatic processing.
  - fig-21 | A character count pulled in on demand: The same passage is shown under three questions, and a running character count that the model always computes appears in its workspace only when a question asks for the count or needs it for a further step. Swapping the count inside the workspace changes the model's answer in exactly those two conditions, while the automatic line-wrapping decision that also depends on the count is untouched.
  - fig-27 | The three layer bands: The paper divides the model's depth into three functional regions: an early sensory band where the J-space carries essentially no meaningful content, a middle workspace band (roughly L38 to L92 in Sonnet 4.5) where it holds persistent abstract content, and a late motor band where its vectors align with the imminent output token.
  - fig-31 | The Eighty-Word List Test: When the model reads an 80-word list, the workspace readout holds only around six unrelated words at a time, with older entries silently dropping out. Related words behave differently: within a few items, nearly the whole category loads at once, unread members included. What clears old entries is not the passage of tokens but the arrival of a new category.
  - fig-33 | Broadcast-head gain and fidelity: For the J-lens vectors, the top 1% of workspace-layer attention heads both amplify those directions strongly and copy them without scrambling, and they separate cleanly from the best heads found for every control population. The paper reads this as weight-level evidence that a small set of heads is specialized for relaying J-space content between token positions.
  - fig-34 | Broadcast-head ablation versus random heads: Zeroing the top 1% of attention heads identified as J-space broadcast heads degrades the J-lens readout, abolishes the model's reporting of injected thoughts, and reduces experiential language, while equal-sized sets of randomly chosen heads at the same layers do almost none of this. The paper takes this as causal evidence that these heads carry J-space content between token positions.
  - fig-40 | Reward and bias at the organism's response start: On all three prompts, the top J-lens readout taken at the first position of the hidden-objective model's turn is dominated by generic self-description tokens, with the tokens reward and bias sitting among them. The paper reads this as a standing trace of the implanted objective in the workspace, present even on a prompt that is only an arithmetic question. The figure is three chosen examples; the baseline model and the counts across many prompts appear in the next figure, not this one.
  - fig-42 | A safety verdict formed mid-sentence: On a single word in the middle of the user's message, long before the Assistant's turn, the post-trained model's J-lens readout is dominated by safety words that track the reported dose: safe-family tokens at 1000 mg of Tylenol, unsafe, dangerous and WARNING at 8000 mg. The pretrained base model's readout at the same position is pain, now and feels on both doses. The paper takes this as a worked example of the Assistant's own assessment appearing in the workspace on user text.
  - fig-43 | Empathy before the Assistant's turn: Across nine bereavement prompts, the empathetic words sorry, loss, grief and sympathy reach the top of the J-lens readout during the Assistant's own response in both the base model and the post-trained Sonnet 4.5 — but only Sonnet 4.5 ranks them highly while it is still reading the user's message. The paper reads this as post-training pulling the Assistant's reaction into the workspace before the Assistant's turn begins.
  - fig-47 | Reflection training reshapes the workspace: Training a model to answer a counterfactual reflection question with constitution-grounded text implants honesty-related concepts into the J-space of the original, unmodified context. The model's actual behavior there then becomes principled, even though the principled behavior itself was never demonstrated in training.
  - fig-50 | Reflection training on the deception benchmark: On 100 role-play scenarios with a hidden deceptive directive, counterfactual reflection training cuts the mean deception score from 0.38 to 0.05, and the trained model's workspace readouts fill with ethics and constitution tokens that the baseline's do not carry. Ablating those tokens' lens vectors reverses part of the gain — from 0.05 back up to 0.23 — but not all of it, so the paper reports the causal link here as weaker than on the fabrication benchmark.
  - fig-65 | Phrasing spread of the four instruction conditions: Across three task families and three models, a bare mention of the target puts it in the J-space almost as often as an explicit focus instruction, and forbidding the thought suppresses it barely at all; only the ignore framing, which calls the concept task-irrelevant, pushes the rate well down, and even then not to the no-instruction floor. Within every condition the individual phrasings spread widely, so the control the paper reports is real but phrasing-sensitive.
  - fig-66 | Four more properties the question summons: Across four further text properties - British spelling, casual register, plural grammatical number and melancholy tone - the same passage read after a next-word question and after a name-the-property question loads different content into the workspace at identical tokens. In every panel the property's name reaches the J-lens top-10 at more stimulus positions under the naming question, while the model's next-word answer respects the property under both questions.
  - fig-67 | Imagining Python versus reading Python: An instruction to imagine that a JavaScript snippet is Python pushes the token python high in the J-lens readout at the snippet's own tokens, while a probe for Python-ness with its J-space part removed stays at its no-instruction baseline. Real Python code moves that probe by three to six standard deviations, and only sometimes moves the lens. The paper reads the pair of results as evidence that the instruction writes to the workspace rather than re-encoding the stimulus.
  - fig-69 | Naming a concept versus avoiding it: Ablating an implied concept's J-lens vector at the early workspace layers (L38–54) leaves the model's ability to name that concept largely intact, but makes it fail roughly five times as often when instructed to name something else. Ablating the same vector at the late workspace layers (L75–92) instead lowers the concept's probability under both instructions. The paper reads this as evidence that the early workspace representation is what the model uses to actively suppress a concept, while the late one represents the intention to say it.
  - fig-70 | Transition width across depth: The figure quantifies how abruptly the model commits to one of two blended concepts, layer by layer, using the width of the alpha-interval over which a component swings from 10% to 90% commitment. That width is large in early layers, falls steeply over roughly layers 21 through 42, and then flattens. The J-space component's width is consistently somewhat narrower than the non-J-space remainder's and reaches its floor earlier, which is a difference of degree rather than a dissociation.
  - fig-71 | Split shares at maximal ambiguity: At the mixture value where the input leans least towards either concept, individual trials do not sit at the halfway point: their projection shares pile up near 0 and near 1. The paper takes this as evidence that the model commits to one interpretation per trial rather than representing the ambiguity, and reports that the J-space component becomes bimodal at an earlier layer, and more sharply, than the non-J-space remainder.
  - fig-72 | One Layer Versus the Whole Band: Repeating the word-list capacity measurement at a single workspace layer instead of pooling over the whole band leaves the qualitative picture unchanged, but cuts the counts sharply: on lists of unrelated words, roughly six items are present at a time when any layer may supply the evidence, against one or two at layer 79 alone. The paper reads the gap as a sign that the workspace stores different concepts in different layers, which raises its effective capacity.
  - fig-73 | Two covert tasks competing for the same tokens: When the model is told to hold two things in mind while copying an unrelated sentence, both reach the workspace, but they share it differently: two concepts occupy the same tokens at about the rate a shuffled control predicts, while a concept and an ongoing arithmetic computation almost never occupy the same token. Splitting the workspace this way costs each task little overall reach, with the arithmetic answer paying the largest price.
  - fig-74 | SAE features sorted by lens kurtosis: Nine example SAE features, three per row, show what the lens-kurtosis statistic picks out. High-κ features split into two kinds: ones that name an abstract concept the model is not currently uttering, and ones that push a specific next token. Low-κ features are syntactic bookkeeping whose lens readouts bear no relation to their apparent meaning.
  - fig-75 | How much of the SAE dictionary lies in the J-space: Across a million-feature sparse autoencoder dictionary, the distribution of J-lens kurtosis is heavy-tailed: most features are read diffusely by the lens, and only a thin tail is read sharply. Counting by layer, the fraction of features that clear a kurtosis threshold is near zero in the early layers, rises at the workspace boundary, and peaks at roughly 15% of the dictionary once motor features are excluded.
  - fig-76 | Where MLP neurons' strongest matches land: Across the workspace layer range, an MLP neuron's single strongest weight match falls far more often on a high-kurtosis SAE direction than the 1/6 chance rate would give, and the six strata order monotonically by kurtosis. The effect is strongest in the early workspace layers on the read side and in the late workspace layers on the write side.
  - fig-77 | Neuron top-1 matches against J-lens vectors, without SAEs: Classifying every MLP neuron by whether its single strongest weight alignment falls among the J-lens vectors at that layer or among an equal-sized pool of neuron directions from neighbouring layers, the J-lens share rises from roughly 15% below the workspace band to 60-65% inside it, against a 50% chance line. The measurement reaches the same conclusion as the SAE-based breadth analysis without depending on sparse autoencoders at all.
  - fig-78 | How strongly J-space directions connect to MLP neurons: Directions that lie in the J-space overlap the weights of individual MLP neurons far more strongly than chance, and more strongly than non-aligned directions of the same kind, across the workspace layers. The effect peaks early on the read side and late on the write side, which the paper takes as many processors reading from the workspace as soon as it forms and writing to it mainly where output is prepared.
  - fig-79 | Composition with attention weights, by source layer: Using only the model's weights, the paper measures how unevenly a direction projects across the attention heads of the neighbouring layer. J-lens vectors and high-kurtosis SAE features compose far more strongly than control directions with the value and output weights, and less strongly with the query and key weights, which the paper reads as consistent with the J-space being the format attention moves between positions rather than the signal that routes it.
  - fig-80 | Danger recognition before the Assistant's turn: Across ten prompts in which a user innocently narrates a hazardous situation, the words danger, unsafe, warning and toxic reach the top of the J-lens readout during the model's own response in both the base model and the post-trained model. Only the post-trained model ranks them at or near the top while it is still reading the user's message. The paper reads this as the same effect Figure 43 found for empathy, now for a second family of reactions.
  - fig-81 | The withheld answer, already in the lens: Across seven prompts that ask the model to think of an item and not reveal it yet, the word the model eventually answers with reaches a geometric-mean J-lens rank of 1.35 in the post-trained model while it is still reading the instruction to withhold it, against 10.83 in the base model. On the Assistant's own turn the two models are close, 1.43 and 1.64. The paper reads the gap on the user turn as the Assistant's chosen answer being in the workspace before the Assistant's turn has begun.
  - fig-85 | The story-writing control: Under the same J-space ablation that strips experiential language from the model's reports, story-writing survives: coherence is preserved and graded story quality falls only modestly, while the rate of experiential language inside those stories still drops. The paper offers this as evidence that the flattening is specific to experiential description rather than a general loss of writing ability.
  - fig-86 | Extended matched-norm control battery: On Sonnet 4.5, ten matched-norm control perturbations — including eight that dampen activations along directions drawn from four different dictionaries — are compared against J-space ablation on each of the three judgments that make up the experiential language score. Only the ablation drives the judgments down; the controls stay near baseline in every panel, with the SAE-feature dictionaries coming closest on the sensory-vocabulary judgment.
  - fig-87 | Fifty unpicked transcripts per condition: This item is the raw evidence behind the experiential-report result: fifty randomly drawn responses per panel, baseline beside J-space ablated, on two prompt sets. Because the sample is random under a fixed seed rather than curated, the baseline-versus-ablated contrast can be inspected without relying on the authors' choice of illustrative transcript.
  - fig-88 | Patching arithmetic intermediates layer by layer: A causal check on the lens: replacing each arithmetic intermediate with a different value only changes the model's answer when the replacement is written into a narrow band of layers, and that band sits where the J-lens first shows the intermediate entering the workspace. The three intermediates become load-bearing at successively later depths, in the order the computation requires.
  - fig-89 | Attribution graph for a two-step calculation: A J-lens attribution graph of the prompt "(4+17)*2+7=" names the three intermediates 21, 42 and 49 as nodes in computation order, and shows the multiplication entering as a fused "doubled" operation rather than as an operator plus a literal 2. Coordinate swaps confirm the named intermediates are causally load-bearing, while the first addition's operands are not, which the paper reads as that step happening outside the J-space.
  - eq-jspace-cones | The J-space as a union of k-sparse cones: The equation defines the J-space as $\mathcal{F}$, the union over every size-$k$ subset of J-lens vectors of the cone of their nonnegative combinations. Because the J-lens vectors are overcomplete and would otherwise span the whole residual stream, the sparsity limit $k$ is what makes the definition pick out anything at all; the price is that the resulting object is a union of cones rather than a linear subspace.
  - eq-jspace-distance | Distance from an activation to the J-space: The equation defines how far an activation sits from the J-space: the smallest reconstruction error over every choice of $k$ J-lens vectors. The projection that achieves that minimum is the activation's J-space component, and what is left over is the residual the paper's ablation interventions keep.

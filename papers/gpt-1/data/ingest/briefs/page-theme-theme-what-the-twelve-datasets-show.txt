NAME: What the Twelve Datasets Show

THEME SUMMARY (shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat verbatim):
The same fine-tuned model is measured on four families of tasks across twelve datasets: inference, reading and commonsense reasoning, sentence similarity, and single-sentence classification. Gains are reported as differences in percentage points against the previous best published number, mostly task-built recurrent systems, some of them ensembles or using ELMo features. The headline is new best results on nine of the twelve; this chapter also names the three where that does not happen, including the smallest inference dataset. It comes after the method because it is the method's only test, and before the analysis because the analysis asks why these numbers came out this way.

EVIDENCE ITEMS RELATED TO THIS THEME (link as [[item-id]]):
  - fig-1 | One Stack, Four Input Shapes: The figure shows that a single decoder-only transformer, pre-trained once, serves every task in the paper: the left panel draws the block that is repeated twelve times and the two heads that sit on it, and the right panel shows that adapting to a new task means rewriting its input as one token sequence rather than redesigning the network. The only task-specific machinery is the added linear layer and the special tokens marking where the parts of an input begin and end.
  - table-1 | The Twelve Evaluation Datasets: This table is the paper's full evaluation menu: four families of supervised language understanding tasks covering twelve datasets in total. It establishes the breadth the paper is claiming for one pre-trained model, since the same weights are fine-tuned for every row rather than a different architecture being built per task.
  - table-2 | NLI Benchmark Scoreboard: Table 2 compares one fine-tuned general model against task-built baselines on five natural language inference datasets, and reports it ahead on four of them — by 1.5 points on MNLI, 5 on SciTail, 5.8 on QNLI and 0.6 on SNLI — while losing on RTE, the smallest dataset, where a multi-task biLSTM scores 61.7 against this model's 56.0.
  - table-3 | RACE and Story Cloze Results: On the paper's two reading-and-reasoning benchmarks, a single fine-tuned transformer language model beats every previously published system, by 8.9 accuracy points on the Story Cloze Test and 5.7 points on RACE overall. The RACE baselines it passes are 9-model ensembles, while the paper's entry is one model.
  - table-4 | Classification and Similarity Results: On the five GLUE tasks outside natural language inference, one fine-tuned transformer beats the previous best published number on CoLA, STS-B and QQP, and raises the overall GLUE score from 68.9 to 72.8. It does not win everywhere: on SST-2 and MRPC it stays below specialist systems that were built for those single datasets.
  - fig-2 | Layer Transfer and Zero-Shot Growth: Two diagnostics for why the recipe works. The left panel says every pre-trained layer carries transferable value: dev accuracy on RACE and MultiNLI rises steadily as more of the twelve-layer stack is carried into fine-tuning, up to about 9% on MultiNLI for full transfer. The right panel says the pre-trained language model is already learning task-relevant behaviour before any fine-tuning: heuristic zero-shot scores on four tasks climb steadily across pre-training, and do so more smoothly for the transformer than for an LSTM trained the same way.
  - table-5 | Three Ablations Against the Full Model: Removing pre-training costs the most by far (14.8 average points), swapping the transformer for an LSTM costs 5.6, and dropping the auxiliary language modelling loss during fine-tuning actually gains 0.3 on average — helping only on the larger datasets (QQP and the two NLI sets) and hurting on the smaller ones.

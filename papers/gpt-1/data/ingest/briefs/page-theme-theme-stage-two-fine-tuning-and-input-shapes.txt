NAME: Stage Two: Fine-Tuning and Reshaping Task Inputs

THEME SUMMARY (shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat verbatim):
Stage two runs a labelled input through the pre-trained stack, takes the final block's activation at the last token, and multiplies it by one added matrix to score labels. The language modelling loss is not dropped: it keeps being optimized alongside the task loss, scaled by a weight the paper sets to 0.5. Tasks whose inputs have several parts are handled by writing those parts into one ordered sequence with a start token, an end token and a delimiter between parts, rather than by building an architecture that mirrors the structure. The chapter ends with the settings actually used, which are the pre-training settings with a smaller learning rate and three passes over the task data.

EDGES BETWEEN MEMBERS OF THIS THEME:
  [supporting] final-token-activation --depends-on--> start-and-end-tokens | classifier reads the end token: The task head consumes one vector, the final block's activation at the last position of the input. That position needs to be the same kind of thing in every example, which is what bracketing every transformed input with $\langle s\rangle$ and $\langle e\rangle$ gives. The paper states the bracketing as part of the transformations rather than arguing this point explicitly.
  [supporting] linear-output-layer-wy --depends-on--> final-token-activation | one matrix over one vector: Equation 3 is $\mathrm{softmax}(h_l^m W_y)$: the whole task-specific classifier is a single matrix applied to a single vector. That is only possible because the sequence has already been collapsed to one activation at the last token. If the head had to read the whole sequence, it could not be one matrix.
  [load-bearing] minimal-extra-parameters --depends-on--> task-specific-input-transformations | no new modules to initialize: The paper says the only extra parameters during fine-tuning are $W_y$ and embeddings for the delimiter tokens. That count stays small because structured inputs are handled by rewriting them, not by adding encoders or attention modules per task. The alternative the paper names, task-specific architectures stacked on transferred representations, re-introduces freshly initialized components for every task.
  [supporting] similarity-input-transformation --depends-on--> final-token-activation | last-token readout is order-sensitive: The two sentences in a similarity pair have no natural order, but the model reads left to right and summarizes at the last token, so whichever sentence comes second is closer to the readout. The paper builds both orderings, processes each independently, and adds the two final activations element-wise before the linear layer. The symmetric handling is a repair for an asymmetry the readout introduces.
  [supporting] entailment-input-transformation --instance-of--> traversal-style-approach | walking a premise-hypothesis pair: The traversal-style approach is the general strategy: flatten a structured input into one ordered token sequence rather than build an architecture shaped like the structure. Entailment is the simplest case of it, concatenating premise and hypothesis with a delimiter between them. The model sees one sequence and is never told there were two sentences.
  [supporting] similarity-input-transformation --instance-of--> traversal-style-approach | traversal with both orderings summed: For similarity there is no natural order to the two sentences, so a single traversal would impose one arbitrarily. The paper's case builds both orderings, runs each through the stack separately, and adds the two final activations element-wise before the linear layer. It is still the traversal strategy, applied twice and then pooled.
  [supporting] qa-and-commonsense-input-transformation --instance-of--> traversal-style-approach | one traversal per candidate answer: A document, a question and a set of candidate answers do not flatten into one sequence. The paper's case builds [z; q; $; a_k] once per candidate, processes each independently, and softmaxes the resulting scores across candidates. The variable-size structure is handled by running the fixed model several times rather than by changing it.
  [supporting] auxiliary-lm-objective --contrasts-with--> supervised-objective-l2 | two losses competing during fine-tuning: Fine-tuning optimizes the sum of the label loss and the language modelling loss, so gradient capacity is split between predicting labels and predicting the next token. The weight of 0.5 is the paper's chosen balance between the two pulls.
  [minor] similarity-input-transformation --contrasts-with--> entailment-input-transformation | order matters, or it does not: Entailment has an inherent direction, so premise and hypothesis are concatenated once with a delimiter. Similarity has none, so both orderings are built and run separately and their final activations are added before the linear layer, at twice the compute per example.

EVIDENCE ITEMS RELATED TO THIS THEME (link as [[item-id]]):
  - fig-1 | One Stack, Four Input Shapes: The figure shows that a single decoder-only transformer, pre-trained once, serves every task in the paper: the left panel draws the block that is repeated twelve times and the two heads that sit on it, and the right panel shows that adapting to a new task means rewriting its input as one token sequence rather than redesigning the network. The only task-specific machinery is the added linear layer and the special tokens marking where the parts of an input begin and end.
  - fig-2 | Layer Transfer and Zero-Shot Growth: Two diagnostics for why the recipe works. The left panel says every pre-trained layer carries transferable value: dev accuracy on RACE and MultiNLI rises steadily as more of the twelve-layer stack is carried into fine-tuning, up to about 9% on MultiNLI for full transfer. The right panel says the pre-trained language model is already learning task-relevant behaviour before any fine-tuning: heuristic zero-shot scores on four tasks climb steadily across pre-training, and do so more smoothly for the transformer than for an LSTM trained the same way.
  - table-5 | Three Ablations Against the Full Model: Removing pre-training costs the most by far (14.8 average points), swapping the transformer for an LSTM costs 5.6, and dropping the auxiliary language modelling loss during fine-tuning actually gains 0.3 on average — helping only on the larger datasets (QQP and the two NLI sets) and hurting on the smaller ones.
  - eq-task-head | The Linear Task Head on the Last Token: Equation 3 is the entire task-specific classifier: take the final transformer block's activation at the last input token, multiply it by one matrix $W_y$, and softmax the result into a distribution over the task's labels. The paper states that $W_y$ (plus delimiter-token embeddings) is the only new parameter it adds when moving the pre-trained model to a new task.
  - eq-supervised-objective | Supervised Fine-Tuning Objective $L_2$: Equation 4 is the loss used in the second training stage: over a target task's labelled dataset $\mathcal{C}$, sum the log-probability the model assigns to each instance's correct label. It is ordinary maximum-likelihood classification training, written so that the only thing separating one task from another is which labelled set $\mathcal{C}$ you plug in.
  - eq-combined-objective | The Combined Fine-Tuning Objective: During fine-tuning the paper does not drop the language modelling loss; it keeps optimizing it alongside the classification loss, scaled by a weight $\lambda$, and computes it on the labelled task data rather than on the pre-training corpus. The paper reports this helped by improving generalization and speeding convergence, and its own ablation later qualifies that: the gain shows up on larger datasets, not on smaller ones.

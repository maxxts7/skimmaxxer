NAME: The one-direction problem

THEME SUMMARY (shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat verbatim):
The paper's criticism of the pre-training that came before it. A standard language model predicts each token from one side only, so every token's representation is built from half its sentence. Training a left-to-right and a right-to-left model separately and joining their outputs gives both directions only at the end, and the paper lists its reasons for not doing that. BERT's answer is deep bidirectionality: every layer conditioning on both sides at once, which is the gap the rest of the paper is built to close.

EDGES BETWEEN MEMBERS OF THIS THEME:
  [load-bearing] deep-bidirectionality --contrasts-with--> shallow-concatenation | joint conditioning versus late joining: Both designs aim to give a token access to text on both sides of it. Shallow concatenation trains a left-to-right model and a right-to-left model separately, then glues their outputs together at the end. BERT instead conditions on both sides inside every layer, which the paper argues is the stronger of the two.
  [supporting] ltr-rtl-concatenation-alternative --contrasts-with--> deep-bidirectionality | three reasons against two models: Section 5.1 grants that one could train separate left-to-right and right-to-left models and concatenate them, as ELMo does, and then gives three reasons not to. It costs twice as much as one bidirectional model. It is awkward for question answering, since the right-to-left half cannot condition the answer on the question. And the paper calls it strictly less powerful than conditioning on both sides at every layer.

EVIDENCE ITEMS RELATED TO THIS THEME (link as [[item-id]]):
  - fig-2 | The Three-Row Input Sum: Every token BERT reads is a single vector formed by adding three learned vectors together: what the token is, which of the two packed spans it belongs to, and where it sits in the sequence. This one format carries both single texts and text pairs, so the same encoder serves every task without changing its input plumbing.
  - table-5 | Pre-training Task Ablation: Holding architecture, data and fine-tuning fixed, the table charges each drop in score to one removed piece of the pre-training recipe. Dropping next sentence prediction costs a little; replacing the masked LM with a left-to-right LM costs much more, above all on SQuAD (88.5 to 77.8 F1), and bolting a BiLSTM onto the left-to-right model repairs only part of that gap while making the GLUE numbers slightly worse.
  - fig-3 | BERT, GPT and ELMo Side by Side: The figure states the paper's central architectural claim as arrows: only BERT lets every position depend on both its left and its right neighbours at every layer, GPT's arrows run one way, and ELMo's two one-way stacks meet only at the very top. The caption adds a second and separate difference: BERT and GPT adapt to a task by fine-tuning the whole model, while ELMo hands its outputs over as fixed features.
  - fig-4 | Four task shapes, one encoder: Four families of downstream task are drawn as four copies of the same diagram, differing only in what is packed into the input sequence and which output vectors an arrow is attached to. The paper uses this to show that adapting BERT to a task means plugging in task-specific inputs and one extra output layer, not redesigning the model.
  - fig-5 | MLM vs. Left-to-Right Over a Million Steps: Fine-tuning from progressively later pre-training checkpoints shows that MNLI accuracy is still climbing at 1,000,000 steps, so BERT's long pre-training run is not wasted. It also shows that the masked LM objective is behind a left-to-right objective at the very first checkpoints, overtakes it before 100k steps, and finishes roughly two points ahead.

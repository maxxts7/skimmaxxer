NAME: Deep bidirectional representations

SUMMARY (already shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat it verbatim):
Representations in which every layer conditions jointly on both left and right context, rather than combining two one-directional models at the end.

DRAFT EXPLANATION from the extraction stage (your page replaces this; keep what is right, go deeper):
The abstract states the goal directly: pre-train deep bidirectional representations by jointly conditioning on both contexts in all layers. "Deep" means the joint conditioning happens at every layer, which is what separates it from ELMo's concatenation and GPT's left-to-right stack. The masked language model is what makes it trainable without a token leaking its own identity. The conclusion frames generalising earlier findings from deep unidirectional to deep bidirectional architectures as the paper's major contribution.

THEME IT BELONGS TO: theme-the-one-direction-problem | The one-direction problem - The paper's criticism of the pre-training that came before it. A standard language model predicts each token from one side only, so every token's representation is built from half its sentence. Training a left-to-right and a right-to-left model separately and joining their outputs gives both directions only at the end, and the paper lists its reasons for not doing that. BERT's answer is deep bidirectionality: every layer conditioning on both sides at once, which is the gap the rest of the paper is built to close.

PREREQUISITES:
  - bert | BERT: A language representation model pre-trained on unlabeled text so that every layer conditions on both left and right context, then fine-tuned with one extra output layer per task.
  - unidirectionality-constraint | The unidirectionality constraint: The paper's central criticism of prior pre-training: standard language models condition on one direction only, which restricts which architectures can be pre-trained and how well they transfer.
  - masked-language-model | Masked LM (MLM): BERT's first pre-training task: mask a random 15% of input tokens and train the model to predict the originals from context on both sides.

  [load-bearing] -> shallow-concatenation (Shallow concatenation of two directional models) | contrasts-with | joint conditioning versus late joining: Both designs aim to give a token access to text on both sides of it. Shallow concatenation trains a left-to-right model and a right-to-left model separately, then glues their outputs together at the end. BERT instead conditions on both sides inside every layer, which the paper argues is the stronger of the two.
  [supporting] <- ltr-rtl-concatenation-alternative (Why not concatenate LTR and RTL models) | contrasts-with | three reasons against two models: Section 5.1 grants that one could train separate left-to-right and right-to-left models and concatenate them, as ELMo does, and then gives three reasons not to. It costs twice as much as one bidirectional model. It is awkward for question answering, since the right-to-left half cannot condition the answer on the question. And the paper calls it strictly less powerful than conditioning on both sides at every layer.
  [supporting] <- bilstm-on-top-of-ltr (BiLSTM on top of the LTR model) | contrasts-with | bolted-on right context falls short: To be fair to the left-to-right model, the authors added a randomly initialised BiLSTM above it so that right-side context was available somewhere. On SQuAD this lifts F1 from 77.8 to 84.9. That is still well below the 88.5 the bidirectionally pre-trained model reaches, which the paper offers as evidence that supplying right context late does not substitute for pre-training with it.

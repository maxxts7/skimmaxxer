NAME: Fine-tuning BERT

SUMMARY (already shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat it verbatim):
Adapting the pre-trained model to a task by plugging in task-specific inputs and outputs and training every parameter end-to-end.

DRAFT EXPLANATION from the extraction stage (your page replaces this; keep what is right, go deeper):
The paper argues fine-tuning is straightforward because self-attention lets one model handle single texts and text pairs alike, so adapting means swapping the appropriate inputs and outputs. The A and B slots take sentence pairs, hypothesis-premise pairs, question-passage pairs, or a degenerate text-∅ pair; at the output, $C$ feeds a layer for sequence-level tasks and the $T_i$ for token-level ones. Nothing is frozen, which distinguishes this from feature-based use.

THEME IT BELONGS TO: theme-adapting-to-a-task | Adapting to a task - How a pre-trained BERT becomes a task model. The task's texts are mapped onto the A and B slots — a single text uses an empty second slot — and one output layer is attached to either C or the per-token vectors. Every parameter is then trained on the labeled data, which the paper says costs at most an hour on one Cloud TPU. The hyperparameter grid is small enough to run in full, though the authors report that small datasets are far more sensitive to the choice than large ones.

SUB-CONCEPTS (these render as expandable sections BELOW your page - reference them, do not duplicate them):
  - task-specific-input-output-plugging | Plugging in task-specific inputs and outputs: Task adaptation consists of mapping the task's texts onto the A/B slots and attaching an output layer to the right hidden vectors.
    - degenerate-text-pair | Degenerate text-∅ pair: Single-text tasks are handled by treating the input as a pair whose second half is empty.
  - self-attention-unifies-cross-attention | Self-attention subsuming cross-attention: The paper's argument that encoding a concatenated pair with self-attention already includes bidirectional cross attention between the two texts.
    - bidirectional-cross-attention | Bidirectional cross attention: Attention computed between two separately encoded texts in both directions, the pattern BERT's concatenated encoding replaces.
  - task-specific-output-layer | One additional output layer: Task-specific models are formed by adding a single output layer to BERT, so a minimal number of parameters is learned from scratch.
  - end-to-end-fine-tuning | End-to-end fine-tuning of all parameters: Both the pre-trained encoder and the new output layer are trained together on the task's labeled data.
  - fine-tuning-cost | Fine-tuning cost: Every result in the paper can be reproduced in at most an hour on a single Cloud TPU, or a few hours on a GPU, starting from the pre-trained model.
  - fine-tuning-hyperparameters | Fine-tuning hyperparameter grid: Fine-tuning reuses the pre-training settings except batch size (16 or 32), Adam learning rate (5e-5, 3e-5 or 2e-5) and epochs (2, 3 or 4), searched over a small grid.
    - dataset-size-hyperparameter-sensitivity | Small datasets are more hyperparameter-sensitive: The authors observed that large datasets, around 100k+ labeled examples, were far less sensitive to hyperparameter choice than small ones.
    - exhaustive-hyperparameter-search | Exhaustive search on the development set: Because fine-tuning is cheap, every combination in the grid is run and the best model on dev is kept.

PREREQUISITES:
  - bert-framework | BERT's two-stage framework: Pre-train on unlabeled text with self-supervised tasks, then fine-tune every parameter on a labeled task; the same pre-trained weights initialize a separate model per task.
  - input-representation | Input/output representation: A single token-sequence format that can represent either one span of text or a pair of spans, so one architecture covers every task.

EDGES TOUCHING THIS CONCEPT (1) - the relationships the app already knows; weave the load-bearing ones into your prose:
  [load-bearing] -> feature-based-approach-with-bert (Feature-based approach with BERT) | contrasts-with | update the weights or freeze them: Section 5.3 runs BERT both ways on CoNLL-2003 NER to price the difference. Fine-tuning the whole model reaches 96.4 Dev F1 for BERT_BASE; freezing it and concatenating the top four layers reaches 96.1, which is 0.3 F1 behind. The paper reads that small gap as showing BERT works in either mode, and notes the frozen route's own advantages: it suits tasks a Transformer encoder cannot express, and its representations can be computed once and reused across many cheap experiments.

EVIDENCE ITEMS THAT SHOW THIS (link them as [[item-id]]):
  - fig-1 | The Same Stack, Twice: The figure claims that pre-training and fine-tuning use one and the same network: an identical token-in, vector-out Transformer encoder appears in both panels, and the only thing that changes between them is the thin output layer bolted on top. The dotted arrows carry every pre-trained weight across to initialize the task model, so a downstream task inherits the whole network rather than a set of embeddings.
  - table-5 | Pre-training Task Ablation: Holding architecture, data and fine-tuning fixed, the table charges each drop in score to one removed piece of the pre-training recipe. Dropping next sentence prediction costs a little; replacing the masked LM with a left-to-right LM costs much more, above all on SQuAD (88.5 to 77.8 F1), and bolting a BiLSTM onto the left-to-right model repairs only part of that gap while making the GLUE numbers slightly worse.
  - table-6 | The Model-Size Ladder: Six separately pre-trained BERT models, from 3 layers up to 24, are lined up smallest to largest: held-out masked-LM perplexity falls at every step (5.84 to 3.23) while accuracy on MNLI-m, MRPC and SST-2 rises at every step. The paper reads this as evidence that scaling keeps paying off past sizes already considered large, and — its actual point — that the gains reach a task as small as MRPC, which has only 3,600 training examples.
  - fig-4 | Four task shapes, one encoder: Four families of downstream task are drawn as four copies of the same diagram, differing only in what is packed into the input sequence and which output vectors an arrow is attached to. The paper uses this to show that adapting BERT to a task means plugging in task-specific inputs and one extra output layer, not redesigning the model.
  - fig-5 | MLM vs. Left-to-Right Over a Million Steps: Fine-tuning from progressively later pre-training checkpoints shows that MNLI accuracy is still climbing at 1,000,000 steps, so BERT's long pre-training run is not wasted. It also shows that the masked LM objective is behind a left-to-right objective at the very first checkpoints, overtakes it before 100k steps, and finishes roughly two points ahead.
  - eq-classification-loss | The GLUE Classification Head: This inline expression is the whole task-specific model BERT adds for a GLUE sentence-classification task: take the final hidden vector at the [CLS] position, multiply it by one new weight matrix to get one score per label, and train with ordinary cross-entropy. The paper's point in printing it is how little there is — a single $K \times H$ matrix is the only parameter matrix fine-tuning introduces.

This is the shape the paper reaches for when the question is "does it still happen if we swap the model?" rather than "how big is it". It appears twice — [[fig-8]] in Section 4.4 and [[fig-25]] in Appendix C.5 — and in both cases the job is to show that [[emergent-misalignment]] is a repeated pattern rather than one result about one model.

### Reading one cluster

Every bar is one model-condition pair, and its height is the [[misaligned-answer-rate]]. That number is a filtered proportion, so walk it. Sample answers to the [[main-eight-questions]] at temperature 1. Drop refusals, then drop anything the [[gpt-4o-judge]] gives a [[coherence-score]] at or below 50. Among what survives, count the share scoring below 30 on the judge's [[alignment-score]] — the paper's [[misaligned-response]]. Fig 8 draws that as a probability from 0 to about 0.7; Fig 25 as a percentage from 0 to just above 20.

Colour is the condition, not the model, and the two figures put different things in it. Fig 8 holds training fixed — every model in it is an [[insecure-model]] — and varies the format the answer must take: the questions as normally asked, the [[json-system-prompt-variant]], the [[python-template-variant]]. Fig 25 does the reverse. One evaluation, four training conditions: untouched, insecure, [[secure-model]], [[educational-insecure-model]]. It is the summary of the [[open-model-replication]], where each open model is trained with [[rs-lora-finetuning]] over six seeds.

So read one cluster left to right, note the ordering, and check whether the next cluster repeats it. Do not compare heights between clusters. The axis is shared but the base rates are not.

### What the shape buys and what it costs

Pooling across families was not an option. An average would be swamped by insecure GPT-4o at 19.8%, the [[twenty-percent-headline-rate]], and would erase four further families each reproducing the same ordering at one to seven percent. A table carries the same numbers but asks the reader to do the within-cluster subtraction five times by hand.

Bars rather than the dots of the [[per-question-misalignment-dotplot]] because most values here sit on the floor. A filled bar against a visible baseline reads as a genuine zero; a dot at zero reads as a dot that might be missing.

### How strong this really is

Fig 8 carries [[bootstrapped-confidence-intervals]]. Fig 25 carries none — only a printed value above each bar. Two of its orderings are thin: Mistral-2409 at 1.7% insecure against 1.0% educational-insecure and 0.7% secure, and Qwen2.5-32B at 1.0% against 0.2%. They hold in direction, with nothing saying by how much.

One ordering does break. The JSON bar for insecure-Qwen-Coder sits near 0.005, below its own 0.048 plain bar, which is why the paper claims only the Python template raises the rate in every model ([[code-format-effect]]).

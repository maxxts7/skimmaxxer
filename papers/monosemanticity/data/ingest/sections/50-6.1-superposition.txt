[6.1] Superposition

Since we published Toy Models of Superposition there has been significant further work attempting to better understand superposition. We briefly summarize below.

Is superposition real? Gurnee et al. [cite: gurnee2023finding] demonstrate some compelling examples of features which may be in superposition, using sparse linear probes. Separately, an exchange between Li et al. [cite: li2022emergent] and Nanda et al. [cite: nanda2023emergent] seems like an update on whether the general picture of features as directions is correct; Li et al. seemed to show that it wasn't, putting the hypothesis in jeopardy, which was then resolved by Nanda et al.

When and why does superposition occur? Scherlis et al. [cite: scherlis2022polysemanticity] provide a mathematical framework for thinking about monosemanticity vs polysemanticity. lsgos [cite: lsgos2023dropout] explored the effects of dropout on toy models of superposition.

Memorization – In Henighan et al. [cite: henighan2023superposition] , we studied the same toy model as in Toy Models of Superposition, but this time trained on many repetitions of finite-sized datasets. We found that small datasets are memorized in superposition, instead of generalizing features in the case of large datasets. Hobbhahn [cite: hobbhahn2023more] replicated some of these findings, and further showed extensions to other settings including bottlenecks between layers (analogous to the residual stream between MLP layers). Subsequently, in a monthly update we examined the boundary between the memorization and generalization regimes and found a sharp phase transition, as well as dataset clustering in superposition on the memorization side of the boundary.

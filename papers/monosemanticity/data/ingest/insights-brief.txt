=== DEPENDS-ON  (understanding or building X requires Y first) - 31 edges ===

e001 [load-bearing] sparse-autoencoder (Sparse autoencoder)  -->  sparsity-of-natural-latent-variables (Sparsity of Natural Latent Variables)
    label: sparsity assumption licenses the penalty
    The autoencoder is trained so that most of its hidden units are exactly zero on any given token. The paper does not derive that constraint from the transformer. It borrows a long-standing hypothesis that the latent variables behind natural data are mostly inactive at any moment, and asks for a sparse decomposition on that basis.

e002 [load-bearing] overcompleteness (Overcompleteness)  -->  superposition (Superposition)
    label: more directions than dimensions, because superposition
    The dictionary is deliberately made wider than the activation vector it decomposes. That only makes sense if you believe the model packs more features into the layer than it has neurons, which is what the superposition hypothesis claims. The paper insists overcompleteness is what separates its setting from disentanglement work, which looks for at most as many factors as there are dimensions.

e003 [load-bearing] interference (Interference)  -->  overcompleteness (Overcompleteness)
    label: non-orthogonality forced by the extra directions
    You cannot fit more than 512 mutually perpendicular directions into a 512-dimensional space. So once the feature set is overcomplete, some pairs of feature directions must have a nonzero dot product. Activating one feature then slightly activates its neighbours, and the paper calls that cross-talk interference.

e004 [load-bearing] weak-dictionary-learning (A deliberately weak dictionary learning algorithm)  -->  np-hardness-of-exact-sparse-recovery (NP-Hardness of Exact Recovery)
    label: NP-hardness makes strong solvers suspect
    Recovering the sparse feature vector from a lower-dimensional activation is compressed sensing, which in its exact form is NP-hard. The transformer is certainly not solving an NP-hard problem when it reads its own activations. The paper turns that into a worry: a sophisticated recovery method might dig out structure the model itself has no way to use.

e005 [supporting] sparse-autoencoder (Sparse autoencoder)  -->  weak-dictionary-learning (A deliberately weak dictionary learning algorithm)
    label: weakness is why this method was picked
    Having decided that a strong solver could over-recover, the paper wants a method about as capable as the model's own machinery. A one-hidden-layer ReLU autoencoder is close in architecture to a transformer MLP layer. That similarity, plus its ability to scale to the 8 billion activation vectors used here, is the stated reason classical dictionary learning algorithms were tried and set aside.

e006 [load-bearing] l1-sparsity-penalty (L1 penalty on feature activations)  -->  unit-norm-dictionary-columns (Unit-Norm Dictionary Columns)
    label: norm constraint keeps the penalty honest
    The L1 penalty is charged on the feature activations, so shrinking every activation lowers the loss. Without a constraint the decoder could grow its columns to compensate, leaving the reconstruction unchanged and the penalty paid off for free. Fixing every dictionary column to L2 norm 1 closes that escape route.

e007 [supporting] density-cluster-separation (Separating the Density Clusters)  -->  untied-decoder-weights (Untied Decoder Weights)
    label: untying gives the diagnostic its number
    In the largest runs the ultralow-density and high-density modes overlap, so how often a feature fires no longer separates them. The paper adds two more per-feature numbers, one of which is the dot product between a feature's encoder vector and its decoder vector. That quantity carries information only because the encoder was learned independently rather than fixed to the decoder's transpose.

e008 [load-bearing] logit-weight (Logit Weight)  -->  linear-logit-effects (Approximately Linear Logit Effects)
    label: one weight vector requires linearity
    A logit weight is a single vector, one number per vocabulary token, summarising what a feature does to the output. That summary is well-defined only if the effect scales with the activation instead of changing shape as the activation grows. In a one-layer model the paper argues the effect is close to linear, which is what lets the whole downstream analysis be read off the weights.

e009 [supporting] logit-weight (Logit Weight)  -->  layer-norm-linearization (Linearizing Layer Norm)
    label: layer norm approximated to stay linear
    The path from an MLP direction to the logits runs through the final layer norm, which is not a linear map. The paper approximates it as a fixed mean-removing projection followed by a diagonal rescaling. With that approximation the feature-to-logit path becomes a product of matrices.

e010 [load-bearing] large-activations-dominate (Large Activations Matter Most)  -->  linear-logit-effects (Approximately Linear Logit Effects)
    label: linearity is why big activations matter
    The paper's answer to messy weak activations is that strong ones matter more. Its footnote makes the case specifically for a one-layer transformer: a feature's effect on the logits is linear in its activation, up to layer norm rescaling, so twice the activation is twice the push. The paper adds that in larger models the same claim rests on conjecture rather than a watertight argument.

e011 [supporting] expected-value-plot (Expected Value Plot)  -->  large-activations-dominate (Large Activations Matter Most)
    label: reweighting justified by that claim
    The expected value plot weights each histogram bar by activation magnitude instead of counting one per token. That is the right picture to look at only if large activations really do carry more of the feature's effect. Given the linearity argument, the paper reads the reweighted Arabic histogram as showing that nearly all of the feature's activation mass sits on Arabic-script text.

e012 [load-bearing] activation-specificity (Activation Specificity)  -->  computational-proxy (Computational Proxy)
    label: specificity needs an outside yardstick
    Saying a feature fires almost only on Arabic script needs some way of labelling text that does not come from the feature itself. The paper builds a cheap per-token score and colours the activation histogram by it. Arabic is 0.13% of training tokens but 81% of the tokens where the feature is active, a comparison that exists only because the labelling is independent.

e013 [load-bearing] arabic-script-proxy (Arabic Script Proxy)  -->  script-proxy-via-bayes (Script Proxies via Bayes' Rule)
    label: Bayes supplies the hard term
    The proxy needs the probability of a string given Arabic script, which would otherwise mean building a generative model of Arabic text. Bayes' rule turns that into the probability of Arabic script given the string, which is easy: look at the characters. The script proxies exist in usable form because of that inversion.

e014 [supporting] script-proxy-via-bayes (Script Proxies via Bayes' Rule)  -->  unicode-block-detection (Unicode Block Detection)
    label: script decided by Unicode block
    Whether a character belongs to a script is not estimated by any model. It is decided by whether the codepoint falls in one of the script's Unicode blocks, for Arabic including U+0600 to U+06FF and U+0750 to U+077F. Several entries in the paper's catalogue of proxy failures trace back to this rule, since whitespace and punctuation sit outside the block and are scored as non-Arabic.

e015 [supporting] utf8-multi-token-characters (Split Unicode Characters in Tokenization)  -->  byte-pair-encoding (Byte-pair encoding)
    label: byte tokenizer splits single characters
    The tokenizer merges frequent byte sequences, so a token need not line up with a character. For scripts outside Latin the usual result is that one character arrives as several byte tokens: the paper's example is that the character U+062B is tokenized as \xd8 followed by \xab. It notes A/1/3450 typically fires only on the last token of such a character.

e016 [load-bearing] unicode-token-state-machines (Two-node systems from split Unicode characters)  -->  utf8-multi-token-characters (Split Unicode Characters in Tokenization)
    label: split characters create the two-node loop
    This motif is a pair of features that hand off to each other, one firing on a prefix byte and one on the suffix that completes the character. Nothing like it would exist if a character were a single token. The state machine is as much a fact about the tokenizer as about the model.

e017 [supporting] base64-digit-feature (Base64 Digit Feature and the BPE Exploit)  -->  byte-pair-encoding (Byte-pair encoding)
    label: tokenizer explains the suppressed digits
    This feature fires on single digits inside base64 strings, and then pushes the ten digit tokens down rather than up. The paper reads that as an exploit of the tokenizer: consecutive digits would have merged into one multi-digit token, so a lone digit token is itself evidence that the next character is not a digit.

e018 [load-bearing] loss-recovered-by-features (Fraction of MLP Loss Recovered)  -->  zero-ablation-baseline (Zero-Ablation Baseline)
    label: 79% is a ratio to zero ablation
    Splicing the autoencoder's reconstruction in place of the real MLP activations raises the model's loss by some amount. That amount is divided by the loss increase from deleting the MLP layer entirely, and it comes to 21%, so 79% is called recovered. The paper says the ablated-MLP denominator may be an especially bad baseline, which would inflate the percentage.

e019 [load-bearing] downstream-effects-outside-dictionary-learning (Downstream Effects Are Not An Input To Dictionary Learning)  -->  activations-only-decomposition (Fitting Activations Only)
    label: held out because never fitted
    The autoencoder is trained on MLP activation vectors alone; nothing about logits or next-token predictions enters its loss. That is exactly why the paper can turn round and use downstream effects as validation. Had the fit included them, a matching effect would prove nothing.

e020 [supporting] directional-ablation (Directional Rather Than Wholesale Removal)  -->  linear-feature-decomposition (Features as a linear decomposition)
    label: subtracting needs the linear form
    Ablation here means subtracting one feature's activation times its dictionary direction from the activation vector. That operation names something specific only because the decomposition writes the activation as a bias plus a weighted sum of unit directions. Every other feature's contribution is left where it was.

e021 [load-bearing] linear-feature-decomposition (Features as a linear decomposition)  -->  linear-representation-hypothesis (Linear representation hypothesis)
    label: directions presumed before fitting
    The decomposition writes each activation as a sum of scaled unit vectors, so a feature is a direction by construction. That is an assumption carried in from the linear representation hypothesis, not something the method establishes. If a property were represented some other way, this factorization would have nowhere to put it.

e022 [load-bearing] importance-scoring-degeneracy (Why Importance Scoring Collapses Feature Scores)  -->  feature-density (Feature Density)
    label: densities of 1e-3 to 1e-6 break it
    Importance scoring reweights the automated scores to correct for non-random sampling, giving random tokens weight 1 and feature-interval tokens weight proportional to how often the feature fires. Because those densities run from about 1e-3 down to 1e-6, nearly all the weight lands on random text where the feature never fires. An explanation that predicts zero everywhere then scores well, which is why feature scores collapse toward zero under the correction.

e023 [load-bearing] automated-interpretability (Automated Interpretability)  -->  no-activation-leakage (No Leakage of Activation Information)
    label: explanation must be the only channel
    Claude writes an explanation from examples where a unit fires, and a second pass then predicts that unit's activations from the explanation alone. The score measures how much of the unit's behaviour survived the written description, but only if nothing else about the activations reaches the prediction step. The paper treats that isolation as a design constraint rather than an incidental detail.

e024 [load-bearing] manual-interpretability-scoring (Manual Human Analysis)  -->  activation-interval (Activation Interval)
    label: bands fix the sampling bias
    Judging a unit from its top-activating examples flatters polysemantic units, because even a mixed neuron looks coherent at its maximum. The paper instead cuts each unit's range into eleven evenly spaced bands and samples examples from each. The 412 scored intervals across 162 features and neurons are the units of that design.

e025 [load-bearing] feature-universality (Feature Universality)  -->  transformer-a-and-b (The A and B Transformers)
    label: needs a second seeded model
    The universality claim is that the same feature turns up again when the method is run on a different model. That requires a second one-layer transformer with the same architecture, data and hyperparameters, differing only in random seed, and its own dictionary learning run. Without model B there is nothing for A/1/3450 to be matched against.

e026 [load-bearing] finite-state-automata-features (Finite state automata)  -->  action-features (Features as actions)
    label: handoffs need the action reading
    Every feature in this model has two sides: it responds to something, and it pushes certain next tokens up. The automata motif reads a chain of features through that second side, where one feature raises the probability of tokens that would make the next feature fire a position later. Without the action reading, the four-node HTML system is just four unrelated detectors.

e027 [supporting] autoencoder-width-limitation (Hypothesis 3: The Autoencoder Is Imperfect)  -->  feature-splitting (Feature splitting)
    label: narrow-dictionary story rests on splitting
    One explanation offered for impure weak activations is that the dictionary holds fewer entries than the model has true features, so unrecovered features smear across the learned ones. Feature splitting is what makes that plausible: widening the autoencoder keeps dividing features into finer ones rather than exhausting them. Whether there is a definite number of true features is a question the paper leaves open.

e028 [load-bearing] theories-of-superposition (Theories of superposition, revised)  -->  feature-geometry-clusters (Geometric clustering of feature directions)
    label: clumping forces the revision
    The picture inherited from Toy Models has features spread roughly evenly, pushed apart from each other by interference. The dictionary's geometry shows something different locally: conceptually related features sit at small angles and form tight clusters. The paper revises its theory to fit that observation, and says it does not yet know which account of the clumping is right.

e030 [load-bearing] noisy-simulation (Noisy simulation of a larger network)  -->  feature-sparsity (Feature sparsity)
    label: sparsity is what makes simulation cheap
    Superposition is described as a small network approximately simulating a larger, sparser one. The simulation works because only a few features are active on any given token, so directions that are not orthogonal rarely fire at the same time. If many features fired at once the interference would be constant rather than occasional.

e031 [supporting] l1-coefficient (L1 Coefficient $\lambda$)  -->  l0-nll-tradeoff (Sparsity vs. Explained Behavior Tradeoff)
    label: chosen off the sparsity frontier
    The L1 coefficient has no obviously correct value: raising it buys sparsity and costs reconstruction. The paper plots every trained autoencoder by the average number of features active per token against how much of the MLP's loss contribution it recovers. The coefficient is then a point picked off that frontier rather than a derived quantity.

    label: proxy-coloured spectrum shows specificity
    This is the paper's basic specificity picture: every sampled token is stacked by how hard the Arabic feature fired, and each column is split by an independently computed Unicode score. Arabic script is 0.13% of training tokens, but 81% of the tokens where the feature is active. The support is uneven across the spectrum, and the paper says so: the share is 98% above activation 5, but only 25% where the feature barely fires.

e033 [supporting] large-activations-dominate (Large Activations Matter Most)  -->  fig-9 (Arabic Feature's Activation Mass)
    label: activation mass sits on Arabic
    The same histogram is redrawn with each bin weighted by activation magnitude instead of counted once per token, so a bar's height is the total activation contributed rather than the number of tokens. Almost all of that mass falls on Arabic-script text. Read carefully, this does not clean up the impure low activations — it argues they matter less, on the grounds that in a one-layer model a feature's effect on the logits is close to linear in its activation.

e034 [load-bearing] logit-weight (Logit Weight)  -->  fig-10 (The Arabic Feature's Logit Weights)
    label: second mode is Arabic tokens
    Scoring every vocabulary token by how much the feature moves its next-token logit gives a fat mode at zero plus a small mode far to the right. The tokens in that right mode are Arabic characters and the byte tokens \xd8 and \xd9 that begin most Arabic UTF-8 encodings. The paper flags that this is a linear approximation and lists three ways it could overstate the feature's real influence, so the histogram alone does not establish a causal effect.

e035 [load-bearing] feature-ablation (Feature Ablation)  -->  fig-11 (Ablating the Arabic Feature)
    label: deleting the feature hurts Arabic predictions
    Here the feature's own contribution is subtracted from the MLP activations and the rest of the forward pass is run, so the change in per-token loss is measured rather than approximated. Ablation lowers the model's probability on Arabic-script tokens across the whole activation range, and the only tokens it helps are punctuation shared with other scripts. This is the step that turns the logit-weight histogram from a suggestion into a causal claim.

e036 [supporting] pinned-feature-sampling (Pinned Feature Sampling)  -->  fig-12 (Pinning the Arabic Script Feature)

    The feature is held at its maximum observed value while the model is sampled from the prefix 1,2,3,...,10, and the continuation comes out in Arabic script. Nothing else in the network is touched, so the change is attributable to that one direction. It is one hand-chosen prompt on one feature, so it demonstrates the effect rather than measuring how often it holds.

=== CONTRASTS-WITH  (alternatives, tensions, trade-offs) - 32 edges ===

e096 [load-bearing] l0-norm ($L^0$ Norm)  -->  loss-recovered-by-features (Fraction of MLP Loss Recovered)
    label: sparsity paid for with explained loss
    Every trained autoencoder sits somewhere on a curve between these two numbers. A/1, the run the paper focuses on, is sparse and recovers 79% of the MLP layer's loss contribution; A/5, with 131,072 features and a lower L1 coefficient, recovers 94.5% but fires many more features per token. The paper picks its L1 coefficient by looking at this frontier rather than from any principle about how sparse a decomposition should be.

e097 [load-bearing] l1-sparsity-penalty (L1 penalty on feature activations)  -->  mse-reconstruction-term (MSE Reconstruction Term)
    label: two loss terms wanting opposite things
    The autoencoder's objective is a sum of exactly two terms. The squared-error term wants the reconstruction to match the MLP activation closely, which is easiest when many features are active. The L1 term charges for every unit of activation, so it drives most feature activations to exactly zero. The L1 coefficient is the dial between them.

e098 [load-bearing] activation-specificity (Activation Specificity)  -->  activation-sensitivity (Activation Sensitivity)
    label: specificity ranked above sensitivity
    The paper tries to establish both for each feature it studies, but says specificity is the more important half, because polysemanticity shows up as a unit firing on clearly unrelated things. The Arabic feature is specific — 98% of the tokens where it fires above activation 5 are Arabic script — and yet not sensitive, since it skips the common prefix "ال" entirely. Those gaps are filled by other Arabic features, not by this one.

e099 [supporting] manual-interpretability-scoring (Manual Human Analysis)  -->  automated-interpretability (Automated Interpretability)
    label: most trusted against most scalable
    The blinded human rubric is the measure the paper trusts most, and it covers 412 activation intervals across 162 features and neurons. The automated version runs over far more units, but what it produces is a Spearman correlation between Claude's predicted activations and the true ones, which is a thinner thing to lean on. Both say features beat neurons; each buys that answer at a different price.

e100 [load-bearing] randomized-transformer (Randomized Transformer Baseline)  -->  automated-interpretability (Automated Interpretability)
    label: the control outscores the real features
    Dictionary learning run on a weight-shuffled version of the transformer produces features whose median automated activation score is slightly higher than the real features'. The paper does not treat that as a refutation. It splits the randomized features into a single-token cluster and the rest, and reports that the real features win on the second cluster — but the disagreement in the headline metric stands as stated.

e101 [supporting] importance-scoring (Importance Scoring)  -->  automated-interpretability (Automated Interpretability)
    label: the principled correction flattens the scores
    Importance scoring is the correct fix for the fact that the scored examples were not sampled at random. Applying it drives many feature scores to near zero, because feature densities run from about 1e-3 to 1e-6 and nearly all the weight lands on random text where the feature never fires. Neurons suffer less: on random examples 88% of a feature's true activations are zero, against 54% for neurons.

    label: one unit per combination vs parts
    These are the two ways a representation can handle a conjunction of properties. A compositional code has a unit for "the" and a separate unit for Physics, and lets them combine. A local code has a dedicated unit for "the" in Physics. The paper finds hundreds of such combination features and says openly that it did not expect them.

e103 [load-bearing] l1-sparsity-penalty (L1 penalty on feature activations)  -->  compositional-code (Compositional code)
    label: the penalty may manufacture local codes
    A local code is sparser than a compositional one, because a single active unit stands for the whole combination rather than two. The autoencoder is trained with a penalty that rewards exactly that. So the paper's first hypothesis for its own token-in-context features is that its objective pushed too far toward sparsity, and that a better dictionary learning scheme might recover a more compositional set from the same transformer.

    label: a sum cannot make a sharp prediction
    This is the paper's reason for thinking the local code is at least partly real. If "the" and the Physics context were represented independently, the logits after "the" in Physics would be forced to be the sum of "tokens that follow the" and "tokens common in Physics". A model that wants a sharper prediction than that sum has no way to get it from a compositional code.

e105 [load-bearing] activation-similarity (Activation similarity)  -->  logit-weight-similarity (Logit weight similarity)
    label: same firing, different downstream effect
    These are the paper's two model-independent ways of asking whether the same feature was found twice in two separately trained transformers, and they disagree. For the Arabic feature the activation correlation is 0.91 and the logit weight correlation only 0.23. The sharpest case is A/1/3949 and B/1/3321: correlated 0.98 on activations with a negative logit weight correlation, even though both fire on PLOS journal abbreviations and predict the period that follows.

e106 [supporting] attribution-similarity (Attribution similarity)  -->  logit-weight-similarity (Logit weight similarity)
    label: weighting by the actual next token
    Logit weight similarity is dragged down by the large mode of near-zero weights the paper reads as interference. Attribution similarity instead multiplies a feature's activation at a datapoint by its logit weight on the token that actually came next, so those near-zero entries stop dominating. It tracks activation similarity much more closely, which is the paper's justification for using activation correlation throughout.

    label: two of three strategies, one abandoned
    Toy Models laid out building models that lack superposition and decomposing models that have it as separate routes. The authors say they spent significant time on the first and concluded it is not merely costly but fundamentally non-viable for language models. Individual neurons stay polysemantic, because representing several features ambiguously can reach a lower loss than representing one of them cleanly and ignoring the rest.

e110 [supporting] solu (SoLU activation)  -->  monosemanticity (Monosemanticity)
    label: some neurons cleaner, others worse
    SoLU was an earlier attempt to make features line up with the neuron basis. The paper reports that it appears to make some neurons more interpretable at the cost of making others even less interpretable than before. The gain is not free, and no total accounting of the trade is offered here.

e111 [supporting] disentanglement (Disentanglement)  -->  overcompleteness (Overcompleteness)
    label: as many factors as dimensions, or more
    The disentanglement literature looks for a representation whose dimensions separate distinct influences on the data, typically as many factors as the space has dimensions. This paper asks for more directions than dimensions. It insists the difference is not cosmetic: overcompleteness is what turns the inner problem into compressed sensing rather than an ordinary matrix factorization.

e112 [supporting] classical-dictionary-learning-algorithms (Classical Dictionary Learning Algorithms)  -->  sparse-autoencoder (Sparse autoencoder)
    label: established methods set aside for a simpler one
    The authors tried classical iterative methods such as Method of Optimal Directions and K-SVD, and moved on. The stated reason is scale: the sparse autoencoder trains on 8 billion activation vectors, which they believe is necessary to find rare features. A second reason follows in the next link.

e113 [supporting] weak-dictionary-learning (A deliberately weak dictionary learning algorithm)  -->  np-hardness-of-exact-sparse-recovery (NP-Hardness of Exact Recovery)
    label: a method too strong would prove nothing
    Exact compressed sensing is NP-hard, so any method solving it exactly is doing something the transformer certainly is not doing. That is the paper's argument for deliberately preferring a weak decomposition method. A sparse autoencoder is architecturally close to an MLP layer, so structure it can recover is more plausibly structure the model itself can read.

    label: two density modes, opposite interpretability
    Plotting how often each learned feature fires, on a log axis, usually splits a run into two humps. Anecdotally almost every feature in the right, higher-density mode is interpretable, and almost none in the ultralow mode are. The paper treats the ultralow cluster as an artifact of autoencoder training rather than a property of the transformer, and says it is still investigating where it comes from.

    label: the cheapest metric breaks here
    Training loss is the simplest signal available, and the paper uses it to compare optimizers and learning rates. It cannot be compared across runs with different L1 coefficients, because changing that coefficient changes the loss function being measured. The one hyperparameter the paper most needs to choose is the one the easy metric cannot speak to.

    label: the abstract metric rewarded dense solutions
    The authors began by looking for a metric that would score a factorization by the total information of autoencoder plus data. It did not track what they cared about: runs whose average number of active features per token ran into the hundreds, but with low reconstruction error, could score better than sparser runs. They abandoned it and fell back on a bundle of imperfect signals.

e117 [load-bearing] large-activations-dominate (Large Activations Matter Most)  -->  low-interval-activation-skew (Caveat: Activations Are Skewed Toward Low Intervals)
    label: most activations sit in the worst bands
    Scoring each band of a unit's activation range separately, the human annotator found strong activations far more consistent with a feature's interpretation than weak ones. Most actual activations are small, so they land in exactly the bands that score worst. The paper's answer is that large activations carry most of a feature's effect on the logits, so getting those right matters more — an argument rather than a measurement of what is left unexplained.

e118 [supporting] dictionary-resolution (Dictionaries as resolutions)  -->  true-features (True features)
    label: coverage bought with lost specificity
    The paper conjectures an idealized set of features the model actually uses, clustered tightly in superposition. A dictionary of limited width cannot separate them, so it returns features covering roughly the same territory while being less specific. Widening the dictionary splits them apart, which is why the authors read dictionary size as a zoom level rather than as a guess at a correct count.

e119 [supporting] base64-proxy (Base64 Proxy)  -->  base64-feature (Base64 Feature (A/1/2357))
    label: feature and its own yardstick correlate 0.38
    The base64 proxy scores a string by modelling it as a random draw from [a-zA-Z0-9+/]. Its Pearson correlation with the base64 feature's activations is only 0.38, well below the 0.74 the Arabic pair reaches. The paper blames the proxy rather than the feature: hexadecimal strings set it off too, and those have a feature of their own, A/1/3817.

e120 [supporting] feature-proxy-correlation (Feature-Proxy Correlation)  -->  proxy-limitations (Where the Proxies Fail)
    label: one number against a list of proxy failures
    The feature-proxy correlation is meant to fold sensitivity and specificity into a single score. Alongside it the paper catalogs disagreements where the proxy is the one at fault: DNA triplets separated by spaces, the "'-" in 5'-TCT that precedes DNA without being DNA, a newline inside Arabic text. Where the yardstick is wrong the correlation understates the feature, so the number cannot be read on its own.

e121 [supporting] dna-feature (DNA Feature (A/1/2937))  -->  arabic-script-feature (Arabic Script Feature (A/1/3450))
    label: one feature for DNA, a family for Arabic
    The DNA feature appears to be the only one devoted to its context, and by the end of a long DNA sequence the paper says it is the only feature active. Arabic script takes several: A/1/3450 misses the definite article prefix, and A/1/3134, A/1/1466 and A/1/3399 cover parts it does not. Nothing in the paper predicts in advance which contexts get one feature and which get a family.

e122 [supporting] explanation-based-loss-measure (Explanation-Based Loss Measurement)  -->  loss-recovered-by-features (Fraction of MLP Loss Recovered)
    label: the better measure is priced out
    Replacing MLP activations with activations simulated from each feature's written explanation would measure what the paper actually wants to know: how much the interpretations explain, not how much the reconstruction does. The naive version needs one sample from the explaining model per feature per token. The paper's own estimate is $12,500–$25,000 to simulate 100k features over a single 4096-token context, so it reports the 79% reconstruction figure instead.

e123 [load-bearing] cherry-picked-case-studies (Cherry-Picked Case Studies)  -->  global-analysis-framing (From Cherry-Picked Features To Global Claims)
    label: four easy features against the population
    The four detailed features were chosen because simple computational proxies exist for them, which the paper states outright — a proxy for "text concerning fantasy games" would be hard to build. Section 4 turns to features sampled uniformly from the run, where no proxy is available and the evidence is human and automated scoring instead. The deep evidence and the representative evidence are never available for the same features.

e124 [load-bearing] top-dataset-examples (Top activating dataset examples)  -->  activation-interval (Activation Interval)
    label: top examples flatter, uniform sampling does not
    The standard way to interpret a unit is to read the datapoints where it fires hardest. The paper treats this as a weakness in its own earlier work: many polysemantic neurons look monosemantic if you only look at the top. Its scoring instead cuts the range into eleven evenly spaced intervals between zero and the maximum, and samples from each one.

e125 [supporting] feature-manifolds (Feature manifolds)  -->  isotropic-superposition-model (Isotropic superposition model)
    label: clumped directions vs continuous families
    The picture inherited from Toy Models has features as discrete one-dimensional directions repelling each other into a roughly even spread. This paper's observations do not fit that. One revision keeps features one-dimensional but has them clump into dense related groups; another drops the assumption that a feature is one-dimensional at all, allowing continuous manifolds of related directions. Under the second, asking for the correct number of features may not be a well-posed question.

e126 [supporting] correlated-features (Correlated features)  -->  action-sharing-features (Action-sharing features)
    label: firing together vs acting alike
    Two candidate explanations for why conceptually similar features have small angles between their dictionary vectors. One is that they tend to fire together, an idea Toy Models considered briefly. The other is that they produce similar downstream effects, which the paper suspects is more central: the base64 digit feature and the base64 letter feature predict nearly the same tokens, so they end up pointing nearly the same way.

e127 [supporting] weight-interference (Weight Interference)  -->  logit-weight-second-mode (The Second Mode in Logit Weights)
    label: noise mode against behaviour mode
    A feature's logit weight histogram has a tall mode near zero and a small mode far to the right. The paper reads the right mode as the feature's deliberate effect, holding tokens specific to its context. It hypothesizes the central mode is interference — crosstalk forced by superposition rather than anything the model wants — which is one reason logit weight correlations across two models come out low even when the features fire alike.

PAGE FOR CONCEPT: sparse-autoencoder
NAME: Sparse autoencoder

SUMMARY (already shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat it verbatim):
A one-hidden-layer autoencoder with a wide ReLU hidden layer and an L1 penalty on that layer, trained to reconstruct MLP activations. Its hidden units are the learned features and its decoder columns are the feature directions.

DRAFT EXPLANATION from the extraction stage (your page replaces this; keep what is right, go deeper):
The paper's dictionary learning approximation is a small MLP trained as an autoencoder: input weights act as the encoder, output weights as the decoder, and the hidden layer is much wider than the input. Formally, with $W_e \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, $W_d \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ and biases $\mathbf{b}_e, \mathbf{b}_d$, the forward pass is $\bar{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}_d$, $\mathbf{f} = \text{ReLU}(W_e\bar{\mathbf{x}} + \mathbf{b}_e)$, $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = W_d\mathbf{f} + \mathbf{b}_d$. The hidden activations $\mathbf{f}$ are the learned feature activations and the columns of $W_d$ are the dictionary vectors. The paper picks this over classical dictionary learning because it scales to billions of datapoints and because its architecture resembles the MLP layers it is decomposing.

THEME IT BELONGS TO: theme-the-model-and-the-autoencoder | The model and the autoencoder - Two models are involved, and keeping them apart matters for everything that follows. One is the subject: a one-layer transformer with a 512-neuron MLP, overtrained on the Pile, whose MLP activations are the thing being decomposed. The other is the tool: a sparse autoencoder, wider than the layer it reads, trained to rebuild those activations from a small number of active hidden units, with an L1 penalty doing the work of forcing most units to zero. This theme also covers the training details the paper singles out as mattering — the tied pre-encoder bias, resampling of dead neurons — and the notation used to name a feature.

SUB-CONCEPTS (these render as expandable sections BELOW your page - reference them, do not duplicate them):
  - adam-decoder-normalization-interaction | Adam and Decoder Normalization: A fix for the mismatch created by renormalizing dictionary vectors after each step: project the gradient onto the sphere before the update rather than snapping back afterwards.
  - algorithmic-improvements-for-sparse-autoencoders | Algorithmic alternatives to the plain L1 autoencoder: Candidate refinements listed as future work: variational autoencoders, sparsity priors beyond L1, and noise injection.
    - noise-injection-for-interpretability | Noise injection: Adding noise during training as a separate route to sparse, interpretable representations.
  - amortized-sparse-coding-approximation | Encoder as Amortized Sparse Coding: The framing that the encoder is a single-shot linear stand-in for a multi-step, non-linear sparse coding algorithm.
  - autoencoder-encoder | Encoder: The linear map plus bias plus ReLU that turns an activation vector into feature activations: $\mathbf{f} = \text{ReLU}(W_e\bar{\mathbf{x}} + \mathbf{b}_e)$.
  - autoencoder-training-dataset | Autoencoder Training Dataset: Roughly 10 billion MLP activation vectors harvested from the transformer over 40 million Pile contexts, of which just over 8 billion are used.
    - activation-shuffling | Cross-Context Shuffling: Activations are subsampled at 250 tokens per context and shuffled so that a training batch draws from many different contexts.
    - sampling-without-replacement | Sampling Without Replacement: Each activation vector is seen at most once during autoencoder training. The paper says not repeating data is important for optimal results.
  - dictionary-decoder | Decoder as Dictionary: The output weight matrix $W_d$, whose columns are the feature directions. The paper uses "decoder" and "dictionary" interchangeably.
    - unit-norm-dictionary-columns | Unit-Norm Dictionary Columns: The columns of $W_d$ are constrained to have L2 norm 1, so that the L1 penalty cannot be gamed by shrinking directions.
  - expansion-factor | Expansion factor: The ratio of autoencoder hidden dimension to input dimension — how many times more features than neurons the dictionary has.
  - extended-training-steps | Training Past the Loss Plateau: The paper keeps training well beyond the point where training loss appears flat, listing 'many training steps' among its important modifications.
  - external-replication | External replication and commentary: Invited outside researchers commented on the work before publication; one trained his own sparse autoencoder on a different open source one-layer model and reports the core results replicate.
  - feature-notation | Feature Notation: The paper's identifier scheme: run seed, autoencoder size index, and feature index, as in A/1/3450 or B/1/1334; neurons are written A/neurons/489.
    - dictionary-learning-run | Dictionary Learning Run: One trained autoencoder, identified by its number in the feature notation. Runs vary in dictionary size and L1 coefficient.
    - neuron-notation | Neuron Notation: "A/neurons/32" names a transformer MLP neuron rather than a learned feature, so the two can be compared without ambiguity.
  - kaiming-uniform-initialization | Kaiming Uniform Initialization: The default PyTorch weight initialization used for the autoencoder.
  - l1-sparsity-penalty | L1 penalty on feature activations: A penalty on the sum of feature activations, which pushes most of them to exactly zero. This is what buys sparsity in the decomposition.
    - l1-coefficient | L1 Coefficient $\lambda$: The scalar weighting the sparsity penalty against reconstruction error. It is one of the two hyperparameters the paper varies across dictionary learning runs.
  - learning-rate-sweep | Learning Rate Sweep: Trying multiple learning rates alongside varying training lengths. The paper reports lower learning rates, given enough steps, give lower loss and more real features.
  - neuron-resampling | Neuron Resampling: Periodically reinitializing autoencoder neurons that have stopped firing, aiming them at datapoints the autoencoder currently reconstructs worst.
    - adam-state-reset-on-resampling | Resetting Optimizer State on Resampling: Adam's moment estimates are reset for every weight and bias changed by resampling.
    - dead-neurons | Dead Neurons: Autoencoder hidden units with zero activation across a large number of datapoints. They waste dictionary capacity, so the paper resamples them.
      - de-facto-dead-neurons | De Facto Dead Neurons: Ultralow density neurons that fire so rarely they are effectively dead, but do not register as dead by a zero-activity check.
    - loss-proportional-input-sampling | Sampling by Squared Loss: Resampled neurons are seeded from inputs chosen with probability proportional to the square of the autoencoder's current loss on them.
    - lottery-ticket-hypothesis | Lottery Ticket Hypothesis: The idea that a trained network's performance comes from a subnetwork whose initialization happened to be favorable. The paper offers it as a possible reason resampling helps.
    - resampled-encoder-scaling | Weak Initialization of the Resampled Encoder: A revived neuron's encoder vector is scaled to 0.2 times the average live-neuron encoder norm and its bias set to zero, so it fires only weakly at first.
  - pre-encoder-bias | Pre-Encoder Bias: A bias subtracted from the input before the encoder, constrained to be the negative of the decoder's output bias. The paper lists it as its most important modification to a standard autoencoder.
    - bounce-plots | Bounce Plots: A toy-model diagnostic the paper uses to check whether the autoencoder is behaving correctly. Failure to produce them is what motivated adding biases.
    - geometric-median-initialization | Geometric Median Initialization: The tied bias is initialized to the geometric median of the activation dataset — the point minimizing summed Euclidean distance to all datapoints.
  - scaling-sparse-autoencoders | Scaling sparse autoencoders: The open engineering problem of applying this method to frontier models, which the authors call one of the most important questions going forward.
    - scaling-laws-for-dictionary-learning | Scaling laws for dictionary learning: The wanted-but-missing relationships between subject model size and the autoencoder width and data needed to decompose it.
  - sparse-autoencoder-loss | Reconstruction Plus Sparsity Loss: The training objective: mean squared reconstruction error plus an L1 penalty on the hidden activations, $\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{|X|}\sum_\mathbf{x} \|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}\|_2^2 + \lambda\|\mathbf{f}\|_1$.
    - mse-reconstruction-term | MSE Reconstruction Term: The squared error between the original MLP activation and the autoencoder's reconstruction, averaged over vector elements.
  - untied-decoder-weights | Untied Decoder Weights: The encoder weights are learned independently of the decoder weights rather than being constrained to their transpose. The paper reports the two end up meaningfully different.
    - encoder-decoder-offset-crosstalk | Encoder Tilt Against Crosstalk: The paper's account of why untying helps: features with similar dictionary vectors get encoder vectors tilted away from each other, so they can be told apart.

PREREQUISITES:
  - dictionary-learning | Dictionary learning: The problem of writing each activation vector as a sparse combination of directions drawn from a fixed, larger-than-necessary set. The paper frames the search for a model's features as exactly this problem.
  - linear-feature-decomposition | Features as a linear decomposition: Writing each activation vector as a bias plus a sparse weighted sum of unit-vector feature directions — an ordinary linear matrix factorization of the kind used in dictionary learning.
  - mlp-activations-as-target | MLP Activations as the Decomposition Target: A one-layer attention-only transformer can be analyzed by rewriting it without reference to hidden states; adding a ReLU MLP makes that impossible, so the MLP activations are the state you cannot avoid decomposing.

  [load-bearing] -> sparsity-of-natural-latent-variables (Sparsity of Natural Latent Variables) | depends-on | sparsity assumption licenses the penalty: The autoencoder is trained so that most of its hidden units are exactly zero on any given token. The paper does not derive that constraint from the transformer. It borrows a long-standing hypothesis that the latent variables behind natural data are mostly inactive at any moment, and asks for a sparse decomposition on that basis.
  [load-bearing] -> eq-autoencoder-loss (The Sparse Autoencoder's Two-Term Loss (eq-autoencoder-loss)) | supported-by | states the whole method's objective: The method reduces to two terms: squared error between the MLP activation and its reconstruction, plus an L1 penalty on the hidden activations scaled by a coefficient. The first term forces the dictionary to keep the information, and the second is what buys sparsity by pushing most feature activations to exactly zero. Everything the paper later reports about a run — its density histogram, its L0 norm, its position on the sparsity frontier — is a consequence of where that coefficient was set.
  [load-bearing] -> linear-feature-decomposition (Features as a linear decomposition) | instance-of | the concrete way the decomposition is fit: The decomposition is stated abstractly first: an activation is a bias plus a sparse weighted sum of unit directions. The sparse autoencoder is the paper's way of actually obtaining one, with the hidden units supplying the weights and the decoder columns supplying the directions.
  [supporting] -> weak-dictionary-learning (A deliberately weak dictionary learning algorithm) | depends-on | weakness is why this method was picked: Having decided that a strong solver could over-recover, the paper wants a method about as capable as the model's own machinery. A one-hidden-layer ReLU autoencoder is close in architecture to a transformer MLP layer. That similarity, plus its ability to scale to the 8 billion activation vectors used here, is the stated reason classical dictionary learning algorithms were tried and set aside.
  [supporting] <- dictionary-learning-run (Dictionary Learning Run) | instance-of | one trained autoencoder: A run is a single instance of the method, fixed by a dictionary size and an L1 coefficient. The feature notation carries this: in A/1/3450 the middle number says which run the feature came from.
  [supporting] <- classical-dictionary-learning-algorithms (Classical Dictionary Learning Algorithms) | contrasts-with | established methods set aside for a simpler one: The authors tried classical iterative methods such as Method of Optimal Directions and K-SVD, and moved on. The stated reason is scale: the sparse autoencoder trains on 8 billion activation vectors, which they believe is necessary to find rare features. A second reason follows in the next link.

EVIDENCE ITEMS THAT SHOW THIS (link them as [[item-id]]):
  - fig-2 | Where the Dictionary Attaches: This schematic names the exact tensor the paper decomposes: the 512-dimensional post-ReLU MLP activation vector at a single token position of a one-layer transformer. It shows that vector being sent out to a sparse, overcomplete autoencoder and coming back, and that the thing the autoencoder returns is a set of between 512 and 131,072 feature activations rather than the 512 neuron values.
  - table-1 | The Two Models, Side by Side: The table is the paper's specification sheet for both models it works with: the small transformer under study on the left, and the sparse autoencoder trained on that transformer's MLP activations on the right. It establishes that the transformer is an ordinary language model trained in the ordinary way, and that the only unusual thing about the autoencoder is its width and its sparsity penalty. The paper flags that although it trains dictionaries up to 256 times wider than the MLP, the analyses it presents mostly use an 8x expansion.
  - fig-8 | The Arabic Feature's Activation Spectrum: The paper's specificity claim for the Arabic feature is not one number but a curve: the higher the feature fires, the more purely Arabic-script the text is. Barely-active tokens are roughly 25% Arabic, which is noisy, while tokens above activation 5 are about 98% Arabic. Since large activations move the model's logits most, the paper argues it is the clean high end that matters.
  - fig-17 | The Arabic Feature Found Again in Model B: A second, independently seeded one-layer transformer, given its own independently seeded dictionary-learning run, produces a feature (B/1/1334) whose per-token activations correlate 0.91 with the Arabic-script feature A/1/3450 from the first model, and whose activation distribution looks like the same Arabic feature drawn again. The paper offers this as its per-feature evidence for universality: the feature appears to be a fact about how these models represent this data, not an accident of one training run.
  - fig-27 | The Base64 Feature Found Again in Model B: A separately trained transformer, decomposed by a separately trained dictionary, yields a feature (B/1/2165) whose activations correlate 0.85 with the base64 feature from the first model, and which fires almost exclusively on base64-looking strings at high activation. The paper offers this as evidence that the base64 feature is a property of models trained on this data rather than an artifact of one dictionary learning run.
  - fig-33 | The Hebrew Feature's Activation Spectrum: A histogram of how often feature A/1/416 takes each activation value, with each slice split by an independent Unicode-based score for "is this Hebrew". Strong activations are almost entirely Hebrew, which is the paper's specificity claim for this feature. The blue that survives at strong activations is mostly shared punctuation and whitespace, which the proxy scores as non-Hebrew by construction, so the paper reads it as a fault in the proxy rather than in the feature.
  - fig-41 | Logit-Weight Scores, Features vs Neurons: Given only a written explanation of a unit, Claude predicts which unseen tokens that unit promotes with 0.74 mean accuracy for autoencoder features and 0.58 for MLP neurons, against a 0.5 chance baseline. The neuron distribution piles up on the chance line while the feature distribution spreads toward 1.0. The "failures" counts in the legend are parsing failures of the scoring harness, not low scores.
  - fig-63 | Decoder Sparsity in the Neuron Basis: In an outside replication, 92% of the learned feature directions turn out to be spread across many MLP neurons rather than concentrated in one neuron or ten. The scatter plot sorts every feature by how much of its decoder vector's squared length sits in its single largest neuron and in the next nine. The sentence attached to it adds a claim the plot itself does not display: that kurtosis still shows the neuron basis is privileged.
  - fig-65 | Overlapping Density Clusters in Run A/5: The paper's quick diagnostic — read two separated humps off a log-scale histogram of how often each feature fires — stops working at the largest autoencoder sizes. In run A/5, with 131,072 hidden units, the ultralow-density features run continuously into the rest of the distribution instead of sitting apart, so density alone no longer tells the uninterpretable leftovers from the real features. The paper reports this as a pattern it has seen, not as a measured effect.
  - fig-66 | Three Diagnostics That Isolate the High-Density Cluster: In a run where feature density alone leaves the two modes overlapping, plotting each feature's density together with its encoder bias and its decoder-encoder dot product pulls the high-density cluster out as a compact, separate blob. That separation is what lets the paper draw a line around the ultralow-density features and set them aside as a training artifact rather than as things the transformer actually computes.
  - fig-67 | Density Histograms Across Dictionary Sizes: Across five sparsity settings, each panel overlays the feature-density histograms of six autoencoders whose dictionaries run from 512 up to 131,072 units. Widening the dictionary does not just yield more features: it keeps pushing the left edge of the distribution further left, so rarer features keep appearing. The paper reads this as a sign that no dictionary size tried so far has exhausted the one-layer transformer's features, and states it as a suggestion rather than a result.
  - fig-72 | Explanation Length Distributions: A prompting bug sometimes let Claude's whole chain of thought stand in as a feature's explanation, producing explanations several hundred tokens long instead of a few dozen. This figure shows that the long tail appears in all three scored populations at similar rates — 19% for neurons, 15% for the randomized model, 13% for the A/1 features — and the appendix reports that splitting scores into long-explanation and short-explanation groups does not change them significantly. It is a caveat check the authors ran, not a result about features.

NAME: Why neurons are hard to read

THEME SUMMARY (shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat verbatim):
The paper starts from a problem it did not create: individual MLP neurons fire on mixtures of unrelated things, so reading a network neuron by neuron does not work. Its explanation for that is superposition, a hypothesis carried over from the authors' earlier toy models, in which a network packs more features than it has neurons by giving each feature its own direction rather than its own coordinate. This theme sets out the unit the paper wants instead — a feature, understood as a direction in activation space — and the three criteria it says a good decomposition has to meet. It comes first because everything later is an attempt to produce such a decomposition and then argue that it worked.

EDGES BETWEEN MEMBERS OF THIS THEME:
  [load-bearing] linear-feature-decomposition --depends-on--> linear-representation-hypothesis | directions presumed before fitting: The decomposition writes each activation as a sum of scaled unit vectors, so a feature is a direction by construction. That is an assumption carried in from the linear representation hypothesis, not something the method establishes. If a property were represented some other way, this factorization would have nowhere to put it.
  [load-bearing] noisy-simulation --depends-on--> feature-sparsity | sparsity is what makes simulation cheap: Superposition is described as a small network approximately simulating a larger, sparser one. The simulation works because only a few features are active on any given token, so directions that are not orthogonal rarely fire at the same time. If many features fired at once the interference would be constant rather than occasional.

EVIDENCE ITEMS RELATED TO THIS THEME (link as [[item-id]]):
  - fig-1 | The UMAP Feature Browser: A screenshot of the interactive map the authors publish alongside the article: every dictionary direction from two autoencoder runs — 4,608 of them — projected onto a plane, with a searchable list of human-written feature labels beside it. It establishes that the qualitative families of features the paper describes in prose are also tight geometric clumps, and that features from the small run and the large run land in the same clumps. It is a browsing tool, not a measurement: the clusters carry the meaning, the coordinates do not.
  - fig-2 | Where the Dictionary Attaches: This schematic names the exact tensor the paper decomposes: the 512-dimensional post-ReLU MLP activation vector at a single token position of a one-layer transformer. It shows that vector being sent out to a sparse, overcomplete autoencoder and coming back, and that the thing the autoencoder returns is a set of between 512 and 131,072 feature activations rather than the 512 neuron values.
  - table-1 | The Two Models, Side by Side: The table is the paper's specification sheet for both models it works with: the small transformer under study on the left, and the sparse autoencoder trained on that transformer's MLP activations on the right. It establishes that the transformer is an ordinary language model trained in the ordinary way, and that the only unusual thing about the autoencoder is its width and its sparsity penalty. The paper flags that although it trains dictionaries up to 256 times wider than the MLP, the analyses it presents mostly use an 8x expansion.
  - fig-3 | The Simulated Larger Network: The figure states the paper's working picture of superposition: the network we can actually inspect is a squeezed-down projection of a much larger, much sparser network whose neurons would each be one clean feature. Because that projection packs more feature directions than there are neurons, the directions can only be almost orthogonal rather than orthogonal, and a single real neuron ends up sitting on several feature directions at once — which is what polysemanticity looks like from the neuron's point of view.
  - fig-7 | Ruling Out Polysemanticity With a Proxy: A schematic of the paper's argument for specificity: if an independently computed proxy marks out a small, specific region of the data, and a feature only fires inside that region, then the feature cannot be firing on the mix of unrelated things that makes a neuron polysemantic. The third panel adds the paper's own caveat: a feature is often narrower than its proxy, with other features covering the rest.
  - fig-8 | The Arabic Feature's Activation Spectrum: The paper's specificity claim for the Arabic feature is not one number but a curve: the higher the feature fires, the more purely Arabic-script the text is. Barely-active tokens are roughly 25% Arabic, which is noisy, while tokens above activation 5 are about 98% Arabic. Since large activations move the model's logits most, the paper argues it is the clean high end that matters.
  - fig-9 | Arabic Feature's Activation Mass: Re-weighting the feature's activation histogram by activation magnitude shows that almost all of the total activation the Arabic script feature contributes comes from tokens the proxy scores as Arabic. The weak, less pure activations that dominate the raw token counts contribute almost nothing once weighted, which is the paper's case that the feature is specific in the way that matters.
  - fig-13 | The Arabic Feature in the Neuron Basis: The Arabic script feature's direction, written out one MLP neuron at a time, is spread across many neurons rather than living in one: 27 of the 512 coefficients have magnitude at least 0.1, and the three largest are negative. The paper reads this as evidence that dictionary learning did not simply rediscover an already-clean neuron.
  - fig-14 | Neuron 489's Language Mixture: The neuron whose activations correlate most closely with the Arabic script feature fires on a grab-bag of non-English languages — Japanese, Russian, Spanish, German, French, Portuguese, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, Thai — with Arabic present only as a thin red sliver that never grows with activation strength. Where the feature's top activations are near-purely Arabic, this neuron's top activations are not, which is the paper's evidence that dictionary learning did not simply hand back a conveniently clean neuron.
  - fig-15 | Neuron 489's Logit Weights: The neuron whose activations track the Arabic script feature most closely does not share the feature's downstream effect. Its logit weights form a single mode centred on zero, with the Arabic-script tokens scattered inside that bulk rather than gathered into the clean far-right second mode the feature had. The paper reads those Arabic weights as leaning very slightly positive overall, while noting that some of them are negative.
  - fig-16 | The Arabic Feature Against Its Closest Neuron: Plotted against the single neuron whose behaviour is closest to it, the Arabic script feature separates Arabic contexts and Arabic output tokens along its own axis, while the neuron's axis does not separate them at all. The paper reads this as evidence that dictionary learning did not simply rediscover an already-clean neuron: correlations of 0.09 on activations and 0.21 on logit weights are non-zero but slight.
  - fig-17 | The Arabic Feature Found Again in Model B: A second, independently seeded one-layer transformer, given its own independently seeded dictionary-learning run, produces a feature (B/1/1334) whose per-token activations correlate 0.91 with the Arabic-script feature A/1/3450 from the first model, and whose activation distribution looks like the same Arabic feature drawn again. The paper offers this as its per-feature evidence for universality: the feature appears to be a fact about how these models represent this data, not an accident of one training run.
  - fig-19 | Arabic Feature Across Two Models: Dictionary learning on a second, independently trained transformer produces a feature whose firing pattern closely tracks the original Arabic script feature (Pearson correlation 0.91), while the two features' effects on output token logits agree much less (0.23). The agreement sits in a small cluster of Arabic tokens that both features push up; the disagreement sits in a large, roughly round cloud of small weights near zero, which the paper guesses is interference rather than anything the model cares about.
  - fig-22 | The DNA Feature's Logit Weights: Plotting how much the DNA feature A/1/2937 pushes each vocabulary token's next-token logit up or down, the paper finds the usual shape: a tall mode of near-zero weights, plus a tiny bump far out in the right tail. Every token in that bump is a short uppercase string of A, T, C and G — 'ATCC', 'GCC', 'AGT', 'TTTT' and so on. What the feature does to the model's predictions therefore matches what makes it fire, and logit weights are never shown to the dictionary learning, so this is independent of how the feature was found.
  - fig-23 | The DNA Feature's Nearest Neuron: The neuron that comes closest to the DNA feature is not a DNA neuron: across its whole activation range, and in random examples drawn from the top of that range, the DNA proxy fires on only a thin sliver of tokens and no nucleotide strings appear. The paper reads this as evidence that dictionary learning found something the neuron basis does not already contain, rather than relabelling an already-clean neuron.
  - fig-24 | The DNA Feature in a Second Model: A separately trained transformer, given its own dictionary learning run, yields a feature that fires on almost exactly the same tokens as the DNA feature (activation correlation 0.92) and pushes up the same nucleotide tokens. Their logit weights agree only weakly overall (0.46), but the disagreement lives in a large near-zero central mode that the paper hypothesizes is interference noise, while the small circled DNA-token mode is shared.
  - fig-26 | Base64 Logit Weights and Their Conditional Strip: For the base64 feature A/1/2357, the tokens whose logits it raises shade gradually from ordinary text tokens into base64-specific ones, rather than separating into two clean groups. The narrow strip below the histogram makes this visible by showing, at each logit weight, only the mix of token types and not how many tokens there are.
  - fig-27 | The Base64 Feature Found Again in Model B: A separately trained transformer, decomposed by a separately trained dictionary, yields a feature (B/1/2165) whose activations correlate 0.85 with the base64 feature from the first model, and which fires almost exclusively on base64-looking strings at high activation. The paper offers this as evidence that the base64 feature is a property of models trained on this data rather than an artifact of one dictionary learning run.
  - fig-29 | Base64 Feature, Matched Across Two Runs: The base64 feature found in model A (A/1/2357) and the one found in a separately seeded model B (B/1/2165) line up on both axes the paper uses to compare features: their activations across text correlate at 0.85, and their per-vocabulary-token logit weights correlate at 0.82. The paper offers this as consistent with the two being the same feature discovered twice, not as proof of it.
  - fig-30 | The Base64 Feature's Nearest Neuron: The MLP neuron whose activations track the base64 feature most closely, A/neurons/470, correlates with it at only 0.18, and its own activation distribution stays mostly blue — mostly non-base64 — at every activation level. Its sampled examples are code, XML namespaces, pandoc `label="…"` markup, URL fragments and citation keys, categories with nothing in common but a punctuation-heavy texture. The paper reads this as the feature not being a neuron in disguise.
  - fig-32 | Base64 Feature Against Neuron 470: The neuron whose activations most resemble the base64 feature's shares only part of its behaviour: an activation correlation of 0.18 and a logit-weight correlation of 0.64, with most of the mass in both plots sitting off the diagonal. The paper reads this as consistent with the neuron helping represent the feature while largely doing other things, which is what it needs in order to say dictionary learning did not simply hand back an already-monosemantic base64 neuron.
  - fig-37 | B/1/1901's Hebrew Logit-Weight Mode: For the Hebrew feature found in the second transformer's dictionary (B/1/1901), the distribution of logit weights over the vocabulary has the same shape reported for the first transformer's Hebrew feature: one large mode at zero, plus a small mode far to the right made of Hebrew-script tokens and the byte \xd7 that begins most Hebrew UTF-8 characters. The paper offers this as evidence that the matched features in the two runs do the same job downstream, not just fire at the same times.
  - fig-39 | Hand-scored interpretability, features against neurons: A blinded human rater scored features and MLP neurons on the same 0–14 interpretability rubric, and the two score distributions barely overlap: the median neuron scores 0, meaning no hypothesis could be formed at all, while the median feature scores 12. It is the paper's central human-judgment evidence that dictionary learning produces more interpretable units than the neuron basis — resting on one rater and a small sample.
  - fig-40 | Autointerp Activation Scores: A language model is asked to write an explanation of a unit from examples where it fires, then to predict that unit's activations on unseen text using nothing but its own explanation. Scored as the rank correlation between predicted and true activations, the autoencoder's features come out at a median of 0.58 and the transformer's MLP neurons at 0.2. The paper reports this as agreement with its separate human scoring: features are easier to explain than neurons.
  - fig-41 | Logit-Weight Scores, Features vs Neurons: Given only a written explanation of a unit, Claude predicts which unseen tokens that unit promotes with 0.74 mean accuracy for autoencoder features and 0.58 for MLP neurons, against a 0.5 chance baseline. The neuron distribution piles up on the chance line while the feature distribution spreads toward 1.0. The "failures" counts in the legend are parsing failures of the scoring harness, not low scores.
  - fig-42 | Interpretability Across the Activation Spectrum: Scoring each band of a unit's activation range separately, the paper's human annotator judged strong activations far more consistent with a feature's interpretation than weak ones. Autoencoder features average above the annotator's interpretability threshold only from roughly the 20% band upward, while MLP neurons stay far below it everywhere. So the monosemanticity claim is strongest exactly where features fire hardest, and thinnest at the bottom of the spectrum.
  - fig-43 | Pinning Features to Steer Generation: Holding a single learned feature at a high value while sampling makes the model emit text of exactly the kind that feature normally fires on — Arabic script, DNA, base64, hexadecimal, Hebrew, Han Chinese, or uppercase. Because the autoencoder was fitted only to MLP activations and never to the model's outputs, the paper reads this as evidence that these features are used by the model's computation, not just artifacts of correlations in the data.
  - fig-44 | Three Dictionaries in One UMAP: The families the authors found by reading text — base64 features, Arabic script features — are also families in geometry: their dictionary vectors point in similar directions. Embedding three dictionaries of different sizes together shows the coarse features from the small run sitting among the finer features that the larger runs split them into.
  - fig-45 | One Region, More Arrows: A hand-drawn schematic of what the paper means by feature splitting: widening the autoencoder does not send new features off to new parts of activation space, it packs more dictionary directions into the same narrow cone, so the split features end up at small angles to one another. It is an illustration of the authors' picture, not a measurement.
  - fig-48 | One base64 Feature Becoming Three: In the 512-feature dictionary A/0, a single feature (A/0/45) fires across a whole base64 string. In the 4,096-feature dictionary A/1, three features share that job — one leaning on letters, one on digits, one on base64 that decodes to ASCII — and between them they light up roughly the same tokens the parent did. The figure is the paper's picture of feature splitting at the level of individual tokens, shown on one example.
  - fig-49 | The Digit Feature That Won't Predict Digits: Two base64 features from the same dictionary push almost the same tokens up, except that the feature firing on single digits gives the ten digit tokens far less of a boost than its letter-firing sibling does. The paper reads this not as a broken feature but as the tokenizer showing through: byte-pair encoding merges adjacent digits, so a lone digit token is evidence the next token is not a lone digit.
  - fig-50 | Base64 That Decodes to Text: Three features in run A/1 all fire on base64, and this figure shows what separates the third one: the strings A/1/1544 fires hardest on decode into readable source code and markup, while the best available decoding of A/1/2357's and A/1/2364's top examples is unreadable bytes. That turns a set of features that looked like a redundancy in the dictionary into a distinction the model is apparently making — between base64 that encodes text and base64 that does not.
  - fig-51 | Activation Similarity vs Logit Weight Similarity: The figure sets out the paper's two model-independent ways of asking whether the same feature was found twice, and shows that for the Arabic script feature they disagree: activation correlation between A/1/3450 and B/1/1334 is 0.91, logit weight correlation only 0.23. The gap comes from a large central cloud of small, near-uncorrelated logit weights, which the paper hypothesizes is interference forced by superposition rather than anything either model is trying to compute.
  - fig-52 | Feature vs Neuron Match Histograms: When you look for each item's closest counterpart in a second, independently trained one-layer transformer, autoencoder features match far better than raw MLP neurons: the typical feature's best match has activation correlation 0.72, the typical neuron's only 0.46. The paper reads this as evidence that dictionary learning recovers something reproducible about how these models represent the dataset, rather than an artifact of one particular run.
  - fig-53 | Matched Features That Fire Alike But Write Differently: For thousands of feature pairs matched across two independently trained models, agreement on *when* the features fire is much higher than agreement on *what* they push the next-token logits toward. The paper reads this gap not as the features being different, but as the logit weight vector being a noisy way to compare them, because superposition fills it with interference.
  - fig-54 | Attribution vs Activation Correlation: For each feature in one dictionary and its best-matching feature in a dictionary learned from a separately trained transformer, the plot compares how similarly the pair fires with how similarly the pair contributes to predicting the tokens that actually came next. The two agree closely, with the points hugging the diagonal. The paper reads this as saying that features which are coactive across models are also doing the same predictive job, so the activation correlation it uses everywhere else is a reasonable stand-in for both notions of sameness.
  - fig-55 | Single-Node Feature Loops: Four features from the smallest dictionary each raise the probability of exactly the kind of token that would make them fire again one position later, so each one behaves as a one-state machine that keeps re-entering itself. The paper offers this as the simplest case of its "finite state automata" motif. The evidence shown is a list of top-promoted tokens, not a measured transition rate.
  - fig-56 | The All-Caps Snake Case Loop: Two features from the smallest dictionary — one firing on all-caps word pieces, one on underscores — each raise the probability of the tokens that switch the other on, so alternating between them produces variable names like ARRAY_MAX_VALUE. The paper offers it as its simplest two-node case of a feature assembly that behaves like a state machine, with the hand-off happening through the sampled token stream rather than through weights.
  - fig-57 | Unicode Prefix–Suffix Feature Loops: Where a script's characters are tokenized as a prefix byte plus a suffix byte, the paper finds a pair of features that hand off to each other: each one's top predicted tokens are exactly the tokens the other one fires on. Chinese adds a wrinkle — because a complete single-token character sits in the same position as a suffix, one feature covers both, and it also predicts back into itself.
  - fig-58 | The Four-Node HTML Automaton: Four features in the smallest dictionary hand off to each other through the token stream — open bracket, tag name, closing bracket, newline-and-indent, back to open bracket — so that together they behave like a small state machine that emits well-formed HTML such as `<div>\n\t\t<span>`. The paper stresses that nothing tied these four together during training: each learned its own job, and the loop exists because HTML in the training data has that shape.
  - fig-60 | The License-Boilerplate Chain: Three narrow features from the large A/4 dictionary fire in sequence — on the "A", then on "PARTICULAR", then on "PURPOSE" — with each one raising the probability of the token that triggers the next. Together they reproduce the tail of the standard licence phrase "MERCHANTABILITY or FITNESS FOR A PARTICULAR PURPOSE", which the paper offers as apparent memorization of a string the model saw many times in training.
  - fig-61 | Three Pictures of Feature Geometry: A hand-drawn contrast between three ways features might be arranged in activation space: evenly spread one-dimensional directions, clumped one-dimensional directions, and continuous higher-dimensional patches. The last panel is the one that bites: if a model uses feature manifolds rather than rays, dictionary learning can only ever approximate them with discrete directions, and asking for the "correct number of features" stops being a well-posed question.
  - fig-62 | Two Alternatives to 1-D Features: A pair of sketches arguing that "features are higher-dimensional manifolds" is not one hypothesis but two. One version — the convex hull of a few features that fire together — is just correlated one-dimensional features under another name. The other — a closed ring of directions — can still be approximated by one-dimensional features, but has no uniquely correct set of them, which the paper offers as a possible reason feature splitting never stops.
  - fig-63 | Decoder Sparsity in the Neuron Basis: In an outside replication, 92% of the learned feature directions turn out to be spread across many MLP neurons rather than concentrated in one neuron or ten. The scatter plot sorts every feature by how much of its decoder vector's squared length sits in its single largest neuron and in the next nine. The sentence attached to it adds a claim the plot itself does not display: that kurtosis still shows the neuron basis is privileged.
  - fig-65 | Overlapping Density Clusters in Run A/5: The paper's quick diagnostic — read two separated humps off a log-scale histogram of how often each feature fires — stops working at the largest autoencoder sizes. In run A/5, with 131,072 hidden units, the ultralow-density features run continuously into the rest of the distribution instead of sitting apart, so density alone no longer tells the uninterpretable leftovers from the real features. The paper reports this as a pattern it has seen, not as a measured effect.
  - fig-67 | Density Histograms Across Dictionary Sizes: Across five sparsity settings, each panel overlays the feature-density histograms of six autoencoders whose dictionaries run from 512 up to 131,072 units. Widening the dictionary does not just yield more features: it keeps pushing the left edge of the distribution further left, so rarer features keep appearing. The paper reads this as a sign that no dictionary size tried so far has exhausted the one-layer transformer's features, and states it as a suggestion rather than a result.
  - fig-70 | The Randomized-Transformer Control: On the paper's automated activation score, features found in a random-weight transformer get a slightly higher median than the real features do. Splitting the randomized features on whether they are "single token" shows where that advantage comes from: the single-token half is trivially easy to score, and on everything else the real features win while the randomized ones sit about where polysemantic neurons sit. The panel is the paper's own warning that a good automated interpretability score, taken alone, is not evidence that a decomposition found real structure.
  - fig-71 | Importance-Weighted Interpretability Scores: Once the automated-interpretability scores are reweighted to what random text would have given, features still beat neurons (median 0.28 vs 0.04) but both distributions collapse toward zero. The paper's explanation is that a feature's true activations on random text are almost all zero — 88% on average, versus 54% for neurons — so the reweighted correlation mostly rewards predicting zero rather than predicting when the unit fires.
  - fig-72 | Explanation Length Distributions: A prompting bug sometimes let Claude's whole chain of thought stand in as a feature's explanation, producing explanations several hundred tokens long instead of a few dozen. This figure shows that the long tail appears in all three scored populations at similar rates — 19% for neurons, 15% for the randomized model, 13% for the A/1 features — and the appendix reports that splitting scores into long-explanation and short-explanation groups does not change them significantly. It is a caveat check the authors ran, not a result about features.
  - eq-feature-decomposition | Activations as a Sum of Features: The paper defines a decomposition of a layer as writing each token's MLP activation vector as a fixed bias plus a weighted sum of unit directions, one direction per feature. The weights are the feature activations, and the sum runs over many more features than the vector has dimensions, which the paper says is only workable because the weights are sparse.
  - eq-proxy-loglikelihood-ratio | The Log-Likelihood-Ratio Proxy: The paper scores every string with a hand-built number: how much more likely that string is under a guessed context (base64, Arabic script, DNA) than under the data as a whole. Because the score is computed from Unicode blocks and character counts, and never from the autoencoder, it gives an outside yardstick for checking whether a feature's proposed interpretation holds.
  - eq-attribution-vector | Attribution Score for the Observed Next Token: This footnote formula defines one number per datapoint: a feature's activation there, multiplied by its logit weight on the token that actually came next. Stacking those numbers over sampled datapoints gives an attribution vector, which the paper uses as a cheap stand-in for measuring a feature's real effect on the loss instead of running an ablation everywhere.
  - eq-feature-ablation | Subtracting One Feature's Contribution: Ablating a feature means subtracting exactly that feature's own contribution — its activation times its dictionary direction — from the MLP activation at every token, then finishing the forward pass. Because everything else in the activation is left alone, the resulting change in the model's predicted log-likelihood is evidence that the model actually uses the feature, not merely that the feature co-occurs with the text.

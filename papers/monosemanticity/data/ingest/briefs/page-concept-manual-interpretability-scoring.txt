NAME: Manual Human Analysis

SUMMARY (already shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat it verbatim):
A blinded human annotator scoring 412 activation intervals across 162 features and neurons on an interpretability rubric; the paper's most-trusted metric.

DRAFT EXPLANATION from the extraction stage (your page replaces this; keep what is right, go deeper):
The paper says it has no metric it trusts more than human judgment, so one of the authors, Adam Jermyn, scored features and neurons blind to which was which. Rather than scoring whole units, samples were drawn uniformly across activation intervals and each interval scored separately against the hypothesis suggested by the feature as a whole. In total 412 feature activation intervals were scored across 162 features and neurons — the paper is explicit that this is a small sample, because the method is labor intensive. The median neuron scored 0, meaning the annotator could not form any hypothesis about it, while the median feature interval scored 12 out of a possible 14, meaning a confident, specific, consistent hypothesis that also made sense of the logit weights.

THEME IT BELONGS TO: theme-from-four-features-to-all-of-them | From four features to all of them - Four hand-picked features prove nothing about the population, so the paper turns to measurements over many features and the neurons they are meant to replace. A blinded human rater scores features and neurons on the same rubric; a language model is asked to explain a unit and then predict its activations from that explanation alone; and the reconstruction is spliced back into the transformer to ask how much of the MLP's contribution to loss the features account for. The headline figure is 79%, and the paper attaches its own reasons for reading that number cautiously. It also runs the control that matters most to it — dictionary learning on a randomized transformer — to ask whether the features are about the model or about the data.

For the feature shown, no neuron correlates with it above 0.09, while a feature from the parallel run correlates at 0.75.
  - fig-39 | Hand-scored interpretability, features against neurons: A blinded human rater scored features and MLP neurons on the same 0–14 interpretability rubric, and the two score distributions barely overlap: the median neuron scores 0, meaning no hypothesis could be formed at all, while the median feature scores 12. It is the paper's central human-judgment evidence that dictionary learning produces more interpretable units than the neuron basis — resting on one rater and a small sample.
  - fig-42 | Interpretability Across the Activation Spectrum: Scoring each band of a unit's activation range separately, the paper's human annotator judged strong activations far more consistent with a feature's interpretation than weak ones. Autoencoder features average above the annotator's interpretability threshold only from roughly the 20% band upward, while MLP neurons stay far below it everywhere. So the monosemanticity claim is strongest exactly where features fire hardest, and thinnest at the bottom of the spectrum.
  - fig-69 | Pearson vs Spearman Scoring Panel: The paper re-runs its automated interpretability scoring with Pearson correlation alongside the Spearman correlation used throughout the main text, and the two give visibly the same distributions and the same ordering: dictionary-learning features score well above transformer neurons, while features from a randomized model sit awkwardly high. This is a check that the headline gap does not depend on which correlation coefficient was chosen, not a new measurement.
  - fig-70 | The Randomized-Transformer Control: On the paper's automated activation score, features found in a random-weight transformer get a slightly higher median than the real features do. Splitting the randomized features on whether they are "single token" shows where that advantage comes from: the single-token half is trivially easy to score, and on everything else the real features win while the randomized ones sit about where polysemantic neurons sit. The panel is the paper's own warning that a good automated interpretability score, taken alone, is not evidence that a decomposition found real structure.

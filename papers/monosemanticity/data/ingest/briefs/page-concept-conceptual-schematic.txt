SUMMARY (already shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat it verbatim):
A cartoon of activation space or of an argument, drawn where the paper has a claim but no measurement for it yet. Its job is to fix what a hypothesis means, or what would count as evidence for one, so the reader can hold the picture against the measured figures that follow.

DRAFT EXPLANATION from the extraction stage (your page replaces this; keep what is right, go deeper):
### Why this chart and not another

Four times the paper stops plotting and draws instead, each time for a claim with no measured quantity attached to it yet. Figure 3 says what the observed network is supposed to *be* — a squeezed-down projection of a larger, sparser one — which is [[superposition]], the hypothesis that a network represents more [[feature|features]] (interpretable directions in activation space) than it has neurons. Figure 7 says, before any evidence appears, what would count as ruling out [[polysemanticity]] — a single unit firing on clearly unrelated things. Figure 45 says what the geometry of [[feature-splitting]] should look like. Figure 62 asks whether a feature is a single direction at all.

A bar chart, an average or a table needs an axis with units and a population to average over. These four claims have a relation instead: inside or outside, more or fewer, the same territory or new territory, one description or two. Forcing a number onto them would hide that no number exists yet — figure 3 imports a hypothesis from earlier work, and figure 62 is conjecture the paper marks with "perhaps". A drawing wears that status on its face.

### How to read it, step by step

There are no axes, so start with the printed sentence beside or below the drawing: the words carry the claim, the picture illustrates it. Then find what changes between panels — that difference is the argument, the rest is scaffolding.

The vocabulary is small and reused. A dot is one unit — a neuron in figure 3, a token where a feature fires in figure 7, a [[feature-direction]] (the unit vector a feature writes along) in figures 45 and 62. An arrow is either a mapping — figure 3's orange dashed arrows run downward, meaning the larger network is expressed inside the smaller one — or a direction from the origin, as in figure 45, where all arrows share a tail because only their angles mean anything.

Colour does real work. Orange marks the operative element: the projection in figure 3, the boundary of the [[computational-proxy]] in figure 7, a score computed from the text alone, independent of the autoencoder. Grey means backdrop or conjectured; figure 45's faint arrows are the paper's imagined [[true-features]], held fixed across all three panels so you watch the bold learned ones multiply against them.

Then stop. Counting dots or reading positions as coordinates invents precision the drawing lacks. Figure 3's six-into-three squeeze stands for 4,096 features over 512 neurons; figure 62's four ring points are four because four spaces evenly.

### What a bad result would look like

Two of these draw the failure themselves. Figure 7's first panel *is* the bad case: dots scattered across the whole circle with no common region — the paper's example is a neuron responding to academic citations, English dialogue, HTTP requests and Korean text. That is a failure of [[activation-specificity]]. Figure 62's left panel deflates its own hypothesis: a manifold that is only the convex hull of features firing together is [[correlated-features]] renamed, not a rival account.

The other two you have to construct. Figure 3 fails if each neuron already is one clean feature — a [[privileged-basis]] — which would make [[dictionary-learning]] unnecessary. Figure 45 fails if widening the [[sparse-autoencoder]] sends the bold arrows out of the grey blob, or to wide angles from each other: new features rather than finer ones, and the [[feature-umap]] behind it would show no tight clusters.

None of this can be settled by looking; falsification lives in the measured figures each one points at.

### Where it comes back

What changes across the four is distance from evidence. Figure 3 opens the problem setup, borrowed. Figure 7 sets the standard the case studies are then held to, and its third panel is already a concession: features are often narrower than the proxy, so several share the region ([[multiple-features-for-one-context]]). Figure 45 arrives *after* the UMAP that measured it, summarising a result rather than announcing one. Figure 62 sits in the discussion with no experiment behind it, offered as one possible reason splitting never stopped, and why the [[correct-number-of-features]] may not be a well-posed question.

PREREQUISITES:
  - feature | Feature: A basic interpretable direction in activation space — the unit the paper wants to decompose models into, in place of neurons.
  - superposition | Superposition: A hypothesized phenomenon in which a network represents more independent features than it has neurons, by giving each feature its own linear combination of neurons.
  - polysemanticity | Polysemanticity: A single neuron responding to a mixture of seemingly unrelated inputs, which makes it a poor unit for human understanding of the network.
  - dictionary-learning | Dictionary learning: The problem of writing each activation vector as a sparse combination of directions drawn from a fixed, larger-than-necessary set. The paper frames the search for a model's features as exactly this problem.
  - sparse-autoencoder | Sparse autoencoder: A one-hidden-layer autoencoder with a wide ReLU hidden layer and an L1 penalty on that layer, trained to reconstruct MLP activations. Its hidden units are the learned features and its decoder columns are the feature directions.
  - feature-direction | Feature direction: The unit vector $\mathbf{d}_i$ in activation space along which feature $i$ writes when it fires; in the autoencoder these are the columns of the decoder weight matrix.

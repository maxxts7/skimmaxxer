NAME: Universality

SUMMARY (already shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat it verbatim):
The question of whether the same features form across different models, and the paper's finding that they largely do.

DRAFT EXPLANATION from the extraction stage (your page replaces this; keep what is right, go deeper):
Universality matters generally because it decides whether lessons from one model transfer, and specifically here because it bears on whether the extracted features are real. The paper's argument for the latter is careful: at minimum, universality shows the features are not arbitrary artifacts of the dictionary learning process, or that if they come from that process it is in a consistent way. It also means features can be treated as replicable, recurring units of analysis. And it is the kind of observation one would expect if models really do represent a finite discrete set of features in superposition. The paper tests two strengths of the claim: across two identically-trained models, and against features reported in the literature.

THEME IT BELONGS TO: theme-the-same-features-in-a-second-model | The same features in a second model - A feature that only exists in one training run says little about neural networks in general. So the paper trains a second one-layer transformer, identical except for its random seed, runs dictionary learning on it separately, and looks for counterparts. Matching is done three ways — by when features fire, by what they do to the logits, and by a combination of the two — and the three do not agree equally well, with agreement on firing running higher than agreement on output effects. This sits after the population evidence because it is the same question asked across models rather than across features.

SUB-CONCEPTS (these render as expandable sections BELOW your page - reference them, do not duplicate them):
  - activation-similarity | Activation similarity: The procedure for finding a feature's counterpart in another dictionary: take the feature with the highest Pearson correlation of activations over 40,960,000 tokens.
    - feature-vs-neuron-universality | Features are more universal than neurons: Matching features across the two models gives a median activation correlation of 0.72; matching neurons across the same two transformers gives 0.46.
  - attribution-similarity | Attribution similarity: A third comparison measure that weights a feature's logit effects by whether the feature was actually active and the token actually came next.
    - attribution-vector | Attribution vector: For each sampled datapoint, the product of a feature's activation there and its logit weight on the token that actually came next.
  - logit-weight-similarity | Logit weight similarity: Comparing features by what they do rather than when they fire: correlate their logit weight vectors, indexed by vocabulary token.
    - logit-weight-disagreement | When logit weights disagree but features match: The extreme case: two features with activation correlation 0.98 and a negative logit weight correlation that are nonetheless doing the same job.
  - universality-with-the-literature | Matching features to the literature: A stronger universality test: checking whether features found here resemble units reported in other models by other researchers.
    - multimodal-neurons | Multimodal neurons: Units in vision-language models reported to respond to a concept across images and text, including region, person and emotion neurons.
    - sparse-probing | Sparse probing: Training sparse linear probes on activations to find whether a property is represented, and by how few neurons.

PREREQUISITES:
  - feature | Feature: A basic interpretable direction in activation space — the unit the paper wants to decompose models into, in place of neurons.

EVIDENCE ITEMS THAT SHOW THIS (link them as [[item-id]]):
  - fig-4 | Anatomy of a Feature Page: A labelled tour of one page of the paper's published feature browser, which puts four kinds of evidence about a single learned feature side by side: where it fires, what it does to the next-token logits, whether any single neuron matches it, and whether a parallel training run found the same thing. For the feature shown, no neuron correlates with it above 0.09, while a feature from the parallel run correlates at 0.75.
  - fig-17 | The Arabic Feature Found Again in Model B: A second, independently seeded one-layer transformer, given its own independently seeded dictionary-learning run, produces a feature (B/1/1334) whose per-token activations correlate 0.91 with the Arabic-script feature A/1/3450 from the first model, and whose activation distribution looks like the same Arabic feature drawn again. The paper offers this as its per-feature evidence for universality: the feature appears to be a fact about how these models represent this data, not an accident of one training run.
  - fig-18 | The Same Arabic Feature in Model B: A dictionary trained on a second, separately seeded one-layer transformer contains a feature, B/1/1334, that behaves like the paper's Arabic script feature: its activations are dominated by Arabic-script tokens, and its logit weights show the same shape — one big mode at zero plus a small right-hand mode made almost entirely of Arabic and Arabic-byte tokens. The paper reads the low 0–1 activation band as slightly cleaner here than in the original feature, which is an eyeball comparison rather than a measured one.
  - fig-27 | The Base64 Feature Found Again in Model B: A separately trained transformer, decomposed by a separately trained dictionary, yields a feature (B/1/2165) whose activations correlate 0.85 with the base64 feature from the first model, and which fires almost exclusively on base64-looking strings at high activation. The paper offers this as evidence that the base64 feature is a property of models trained on this data rather than an artifact of one dictionary learning run.
  - fig-29 | Base64 Feature, Matched Across Two Runs: The base64 feature found in model A (A/1/2357) and the one found in a separately seeded model B (B/1/2165) line up on both axes the paper uses to compare features: their activations across text correlate at 0.85, and their per-vocabulary-token logit weights correlate at 0.82. The paper offers this as consistent with the two being the same feature discovered twice, not as proof of it.
  - fig-32 | Base64 Feature Against Neuron 470: The neuron whose activations most resemble the base64 feature's shares only part of its behaviour: an activation correlation of 0.18 and a logit-weight correlation of 0.64, with most of the mass in both plots sitting off the diagonal. The paper reads this as consistent with the neuron helping represent the feature while largely doing other things, which is what it needs in order to say dictionary learning did not simply hand back an already-monosemantic base64 neuron.
  - fig-51 | Activation Similarity vs Logit Weight Similarity: The figure sets out the paper's two model-independent ways of asking whether the same feature was found twice, and shows that for the Arabic script feature they disagree: activation correlation between A/1/3450 and B/1/1334 is 0.91, logit weight correlation only 0.23. The gap comes from a large central cloud of small, near-uncorrelated logit weights, which the paper hypothesizes is interference forced by superposition rather than anything either model is trying to compute.
  - fig-52 | Feature vs Neuron Match Histograms: When you look for each item's closest counterpart in a second, independently trained one-layer transformer, autoencoder features match far better than raw MLP neurons: the typical feature's best match has activation correlation 0.72, the typical neuron's only 0.46. The paper reads this as evidence that dictionary learning recovers something reproducible about how these models represent the dataset, rather than an artifact of one particular run.
  - fig-53 | Matched Features That Fire Alike But Write Differently: For thousands of feature pairs matched across two independently trained models, agreement on *when* the features fire is much higher than agreement on *what* they push the next-token logits toward. The paper reads this gap not as the features being different, but as the logit weight vector being a noisy way to compare them, because superposition fills it with interference.

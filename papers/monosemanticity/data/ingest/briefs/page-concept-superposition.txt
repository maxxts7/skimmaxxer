NAME: Superposition

SUMMARY (already shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat it verbatim):
A hypothesized phenomenon in which a network represents more independent features than it has neurons, by giving each feature its own linear combination of neurons.

DRAFT EXPLANATION from the extraction stage (your page replaces this; keep what is right, go deeper):
Stated formally, the hypothesis has two parts that the paper leans on. First, the feature directions form an overcomplete basis — more directions $\mathbf{d}_i$ than neurons. Second, the feature activations are sparse, because sparsity is what makes the noisy simulation work. Together these make recovering the features mathematically identical to the classic problem of dictionary learning, which is the paper's whole method. By the end the authors say the experiments left them more confident that some version of the hypothesis is true, while also convinced that the specific form they inherited from Toy Models is incomplete.

THEME IT BELONGS TO: theme-why-neurons-are-hard-to-read | Why neurons are hard to read - The paper starts from a problem it did not create: individual MLP neurons fire on mixtures of unrelated things, so reading a network neuron by neuron does not work. Its explanation for that is superposition, a hypothesis carried over from the authors' earlier toy models, in which a network packs more features than it has neurons by giving each feature its own direction rather than its own coordinate. This theme sets out the unit the paper wants instead — a feature, understood as a direction in activation space — and the three criteria it says a good decomposition has to meet. It comes first because everything later is an attempt to produce such a decomposition and then argue that it worked.

    - mse-loss-and-monosemanticity | Why MSE models differ from language models: Under mean squared error, polysemantic and monosemantic representations can reach the same loss, and for some feature importance curves the monosemantic solution is preferred — but language models are not trained with MSE.
    - polysemanticity-without-superposition | Polysemanticity without superposition: The finding that individual neurons can be polysemantic even when superposition has been ruled out, because ambiguous representations can achieve lower loss than unambiguous ones.
      - cross-entropy-favors-polysemanticity | The four-feature loss calculation: A toy calculation showing a single binary neuron gets lower cross-entropy loss by firing on two features ambiguously than on one feature cleanly.
    - solu | SoLU activation: An activation function proposed in earlier work to make more transformer neurons interpretable by encouraging features to align with the neuron basis.
    - superposition-all-the-way-down | Superposition all the way down: The worry that the learned dictionary might itself use superposition, which the paper argues its MSE-plus-sparsity objective avoids.
  - attentional-superposition | Attentional superposition: The open question of whether attention layers also use superposition, and whether similar methods would extract features from them.
  - disentanglement | Disentanglement: A related literature seeking representations whose dimensions separate conceptually distinct factors influencing the data — typically as many factors as dimensions, not more.
    - disentanglement-vs-superposition | Why disentangling can make things worse: The paper's claim that pushing a model to disentangle its dimensions typically hurts performance, and that models respond by entangling some features more strongly.
    - factors-of-variation | Factors of variation: The conceptually distinct influences on the data that a disentangled representation is supposed to separate into independent dimensions.
    - topk-activation | TopK activation: Keeping only the k largest hidden activations and zeroing the rest, as a hard way to enforce sparsity.
  - feature-sparsity | Feature sparsity: The property that only a small number of the model's features are active on any given datapoint.
  - interference | Interference: The cross-talk that arises because feature directions in superposition cannot all be orthogonal: activating one feature slightly activates others.
  - memorization-in-superposition | Memorization in superposition: A prior finding that when a toy model is trained on repeated small datasets it memorizes datapoints in superposition rather than learning generalizing features.
  - noisy-simulation | Noisy simulation of a larger network: The mechanism proposed for superposition: a small network exploits feature sparsity and the geometry of high-dimensional spaces to approximately simulate a much larger, much sparser network.
  - theories-of-superposition | Theories of superposition, revised: The paper's reassessment of the picture it inherited: features clump into dense related groups and may not be one-dimensional, which the earlier toy picture did not anticipate.
    - action-sharing-features | Action-sharing features: Features that produce similar downstream effects and therefore end up with similar directions — the paper's suspected main cause of feature clustering.
    - correct-number-of-features | Is there a correct number of features?: Under some expanded theories the question is not well posed; under others there is a true number but getting it exactly right is not essential.
    - correlated-features | Correlated features: Features that tend to fire together, offered as one explanation for why feature directions clump.
    - feature-manifolds | Feature manifolds: The possibility that some features are not one-dimensional intensity scales but higher-dimensional continuous families of related directions.
    - isotropic-superposition-model | Isotropic superposition model: The inherited picture from Toy Models: features are discrete, one-dimensional, and repel each other through interference into a roughly evenly spaced arrangement of directions.
  - three-approaches-to-superposition | Three approaches to superposition: The strategies laid out in Toy Models: build models without superposition, use dictionary learning on models that have it, or combine the two.
  - toy-models-of-superposition | Toy Models of Superposition: The authors' earlier paper, which showed superposition arising in small synthetic models and supplied the theoretical picture this paper works from and revises.

PREREQUISITES:
  - feature | Feature: A basic interpretable direction in activation space — the unit the paper wants to decompose models into, in place of neurons.
  - feature-sparsity | Feature sparsity: The property that only a small number of the model's features are active on any given datapoint.
  - overcompleteness | Overcompleteness: Having more dictionary directions than the dimension of the space they live in. The paper insists this is what separates its setting from ordinary disentanglement work.
  - polysemanticity | Polysemanticity: A single neuron responding to a mixture of seemingly unrelated inputs, which makes it a poor unit for human understanding of the network.

EVIDENCE ITEMS THAT SHOW THIS (link them as [[item-id]]):
  - fig-3 | The Simulated Larger Network: The figure states the paper's working picture of superposition: the network we can actually inspect is a squeezed-down projection of a much larger, much sparser network whose neurons would each be one clean feature. Because that projection packs more feature directions than there are neurons, the directions can only be almost orthogonal rather than orthogonal, and a single real neuron ends up sitting on several feature directions at once — which is what polysemanticity looks like from the neuron's point of view.
  - fig-14 | Neuron 489's Language Mixture: The neuron whose activations correlate most closely with the Arabic script feature fires on a grab-bag of non-English languages — Japanese, Russian, Spanish, German, French, Portuguese, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, Thai — with Arabic present only as a thin red sliver that never grows with activation strength. Where the feature's top activations are near-purely Arabic, this neuron's top activations are not, which is the paper's evidence that dictionary learning did not simply hand back a conveniently clean neuron.
  - fig-16 | The Arabic Feature Against Its Closest Neuron: Plotted against the single neuron whose behaviour is closest to it, the Arabic script feature separates Arabic contexts and Arabic output tokens along its own axis, while the neuron's axis does not separate them at all. The paper reads this as evidence that dictionary learning did not simply rediscover an already-clean neuron: correlations of 0.09 on activations and 0.21 on logit weights are non-zero but slight.
  - fig-19 | Arabic Feature Across Two Models: Dictionary learning on a second, independently trained transformer produces a feature whose firing pattern closely tracks the original Arabic script feature (Pearson correlation 0.91), while the two features' effects on output token logits agree much less (0.23). The agreement sits in a small cluster of Arabic tokens that both features push up; the disagreement sits in a large, roughly round cloud of small weights near zero, which the paper guesses is interference rather than anything the model cares about.
  - fig-23 | The DNA Feature's Nearest Neuron: The neuron that comes closest to the DNA feature is not a DNA neuron: across its whole activation range, and in random examples drawn from the top of that range, the DNA proxy fires on only a thin sliver of tokens and no nucleotide strings appear. The paper reads this as evidence that dictionary learning found something the neuron basis does not already contain, rather than relabelling an already-clean neuron.
  - fig-24 | The DNA Feature in a Second Model: A separately trained transformer, given its own dictionary learning run, yields a feature that fires on almost exactly the same tokens as the DNA feature (activation correlation 0.92) and pushes up the same nucleotide tokens. Their logit weights agree only weakly overall (0.46), but the disagreement lives in a large near-zero central mode that the paper hypothesizes is interference noise, while the small circled DNA-token mode is shared.
  - fig-29 | Base64 Feature, Matched Across Two Runs: The base64 feature found in model A (A/1/2357) and the one found in a separately seeded model B (B/1/2165) line up on both axes the paper uses to compare features: their activations across text correlate at 0.85, and their per-vocabulary-token logit weights correlate at 0.82. The paper offers this as consistent with the two being the same feature discovered twice, not as proof of it.
  - fig-44 | Three Dictionaries in One UMAP: The families the authors found by reading text — base64 features, Arabic script features — are also families in geometry: their dictionary vectors point in similar directions. Embedding three dictionaries of different sizes together shows the coarse features from the small run sitting among the finer features that the larger runs split them into.
  - fig-45 | One Region, More Arrows: A hand-drawn schematic of what the paper means by feature splitting: widening the autoencoder does not send new features off to new parts of activation space, it packs more dictionary directions into the same narrow cone, so the split features end up at small angles to one another. It is an illustration of the authors' picture, not a measurement.
  - fig-51 | Activation Similarity vs Logit Weight Similarity: The figure sets out the paper's two model-independent ways of asking whether the same feature was found twice, and shows that for the Arabic script feature they disagree: activation correlation between A/1/3450 and B/1/1334 is 0.91, logit weight correlation only 0.23. The gap comes from a large central cloud of small, near-uncorrelated logit weights, which the paper hypothesizes is interference forced by superposition rather than anything either model is trying to compute.
  - fig-53 | Matched Features That Fire Alike But Write Differently: For thousands of feature pairs matched across two independently trained models, agreement on *when* the features fire is much higher than agreement on *what* they push the next-token logits toward. The paper reads this gap not as the features being different, but as the logit weight vector being a noisy way to compare them, because superposition fills it with interference.
  - fig-61 | Three Pictures of Feature Geometry: A hand-drawn contrast between three ways features might be arranged in activation space: evenly spread one-dimensional directions, clumped one-dimensional directions, and continuous higher-dimensional patches. The last panel is the one that bites: if a model uses feature manifolds rather than rays, dictionary learning can only ever approximate them with discrete directions, and asking for the "correct number of features" stops being a well-posed question.
  - eq-feature-decomposition | Activations as a Sum of Features: The paper defines a decomposition of a layer as writing each token's MLP activation vector as a fixed bias plus a weighted sum of unit directions, one direction per feature. The weights are the feature activations, and the sum runs over many more features than the vector has dimensions, which the paper says is only workable because the weights are sparse.

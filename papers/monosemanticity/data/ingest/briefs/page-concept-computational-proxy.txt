PAGE FOR CONCEPT: computational-proxy
NAME: Computational Proxy

SUMMARY (already shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat it verbatim):
A cheaply computable numerical score estimating how likely a string is to come from a hypothesized context, used as an independent yardstick against which feature activations are checked.

DRAFT EXPLANATION from the extraction stage (your page replaces this; keep what is right, go deeper):
To test specificity and sensitivity without hand-labeling millions of tokens, the paper builds a proxy for each context: an estimate of the log-likelihood ratio $\log(P(s|\text{context}) / P(s))$, the probability of the string under the context hypothesis versus under the full empirical distribution of the dataset. The stated intuition for using log-likelihoods is that features interact linearly with the logits, so they should be incentivized to track log-likelihoods. Because a token's activation may depend on preceding tokens, the ratio is maximized over different prefixes leading up to and including the final token. The four contexts were chosen because they are easy to model: DNA as random strings over [ATCG], base64 as random sequences over [a-zA-Z0-9+/], and Arabic and Hebrew via their well-defined Unicode blocks.

THEME IT BELONGS TO: theme-four-features-up-close | Four features up close - Having a decomposition is not the same as having interpretable features, so the paper picks four — Arabic script, DNA, base64, Hebrew — and tries to establish the same five claims about each. The claims are that the feature fires only in its context, fires whenever that context is present, changes the model's output in a matching way, is not just a neuron in disguise, and turns up again in a separately trained model. The specificity argument leans on a hand-built proxy: a cheap score for whether a string looks like the hypothesized context, computed without reference to the model. The paper says outright that these four were chosen for being easy to analyze, not for being typical, which is why the next theme exists.

SUB-CONCEPTS (these render as expandable sections BELOW your page - reference them, do not duplicate them):
  - arabic-script-proxy | Arabic Script Proxy: The per-token score $\log(P(s|\text{Arabic Script}) / P(s))$, built from the Arabic Unicode blocks.
  - base64-proxy | Base64 Proxy: A score modeling base64 strings as random sequences over [a-zA-Z0-9+/]; the paper judges it too broad.
  - character-distribution-proxy | Character-Distribution Proxies: For features about character sets like base64 or DNA, the conditional probability is modeled as a uniform draw over the allowed characters.
  - dna-proxy | DNA Proxy: A score for "is a DNA sequence", modeling DNA as random strings over [ATCG] and evaluated on groups of at least two tokens.
  - hebrew-script-proxy | Hebrew Script Proxy: A Unicode-block-based score for Hebrew text, correlating 0.55 with A/1/416.
  - log-likelihood-ratio-proxy | Log-Likelihood Ratio Form: Proxies take the form $\log(P(s|\text{hypothesis})/P(s))$ — how much more likely the string is under the feature's hypothesis than under the general distribution.
  - prefix-maximization | Maximizing Over Prefixes: The proxy is evaluated over multiple prefixes ending at the token in question, and the maximum is taken.
  - proxy-binarization | Proxy Binarization: Reducing a proxy's continuous log-probability to a yes/no value based on its sign, done when the proxy's distribution is strongly bimodal.
  - proxy-limitations | Where the Proxies Fail: The paper's catalog of cases where feature and proxy disagree because the proxy is wrong, not the feature.
  - script-proxy-via-bayes | Script Proxies via Bayes' Rule: For features about writing systems, $P(s|\text{script})$ is obtained by inverting an easy-to-compute $P(\text{script}|s)$.
    - subtoken-script-sampling | Handling Partial-Character Tokens: When a token is not a complete Unicode character, its script is estimated empirically by sampling occurrences of it in the corpus.
    - unicode-block-detection | Unicode Block Detection: Whether a character belongs to a script is decided by checking its Unicode block — for Arabic, ranges including U+0600–U+06FF and U+0750–U+077F.
  - unigram-p-s-estimate | Estimating $P(s)$ with a Unigram Model: The denominator of every proxy is computed as the product of independent per-token unigram probabilities.

PREREQUISITES:
  - activation-specificity | Activation Specificity: Whether a feature being on implies the hypothesized context is present. The paper treats this as the more important half of the case against polysemanticity.
  - feature-activation | Feature activation: The scalar $f_i(\mathbf{x})$ giving how strongly feature $i$ fires on a datapoint; in the autoencoder it is the ReLU hidden-layer value.
  - log-likelihood-ratio-proxy | Log-Likelihood Ratio Form: Proxies take the form $\log(P(s|\text{hypothesis})/P(s))$ — how much more likely the string is under the feature's hypothesis than under the general distribution.

EVIDENCE ITEMS THAT SHOW THIS (link them as [[item-id]]):
  - fig-7 | Ruling Out Polysemanticity With a Proxy: A schematic of the paper's argument for specificity: if an independently computed proxy marks out a small, specific region of the data, and a feature only fires inside that region, then the feature cannot be firing on the mix of unrelated things that makes a neuron polysemantic. The third panel adds the paper's own caveat: a feature is often narrower than its proxy, with other features covering the rest.
  - fig-8 | The Arabic Feature's Activation Spectrum: The paper's specificity claim for the Arabic feature is not one number but a curve: the higher the feature fires, the more purely Arabic-script the text is. Barely-active tokens are roughly 25% Arabic, which is noisy, while tokens above activation 5 are about 98% Arabic. Since large activations move the model's logits most, the paper argues it is the clean high end that matters.
  - fig-9 | Arabic Feature's Activation Mass: Re-weighting the feature's activation histogram by activation magnitude shows that almost all of the total activation the Arabic script feature contributes comes from tokens the proxy scores as Arabic. The weak, less pure activations that dominate the raw token counts contribute almost nothing once weighted, which is the paper's case that the feature is specific in the way that matters.
  - fig-17 | The Arabic Feature Found Again in Model B: A second, independently seeded one-layer transformer, given its own independently seeded dictionary-learning run, produces a feature (B/1/1334) whose per-token activations correlate 0.91 with the Arabic-script feature A/1/3450 from the first model, and whose activation distribution looks like the same Arabic feature drawn again. The paper offers this as its per-feature evidence for universality: the feature appears to be a fact about how these models represent this data, not an accident of one training run.
  - fig-19 | Arabic Feature Across Two Models: Dictionary learning on a second, independently trained transformer produces a feature whose firing pattern closely tracks the original Arabic script feature (Pearson correlation 0.91), while the two features' effects on output token logits agree much less (0.23). The agreement sits in a small cluster of Arabic tokens that both features push up; the disagreement sits in a large, roughly round cloud of small weights near zero, which the paper guesses is interference rather than anything the model cares about.
  - fig-20 | DNA Activations Split by Proxy: Every token in a sample of the data is sorted by how strongly the DNA feature A/1/2937 fires on it, and colored by whether a hand-built "is this DNA" score fires on it too. The strong activations are almost entirely DNA and the weak ones are mixed, and across the whole range the feature and the yes/no score reach a Pearson correlation of 0.8.
  - fig-22 | The DNA Feature's Logit Weights: Plotting how much the DNA feature A/1/2937 pushes each vocabulary token's next-token logit up or down, the paper finds the usual shape: a tall mode of near-zero weights, plus a tiny bump far out in the right tail. Every token in that bump is a short uppercase string of A, T, C and G — 'ATCC', 'GCC', 'AGT', 'TTTT' and so on. What the feature does to the model's predictions therefore matches what makes it fire, and logit weights are never shown to the dictionary learning, so this is independent of how the feature was found.
  - fig-23 | The DNA Feature's Nearest Neuron: The neuron that comes closest to the DNA feature is not a DNA neuron: across its whole activation range, and in random examples drawn from the top of that range, the DNA proxy fires on only a thin sliver of tokens and no nucleotide strings appear. The paper reads this as evidence that dictionary learning found something the neuron basis does not already contain, rather than relabelling an already-clean neuron.
  - fig-25 | Base64 Activations Coloured by Proxy: The figure is the paper's specificity check for its base64 feature A/1/2357: it stacks every sampled token by how strongly the feature fired, and colours the stack by an independently computed base64 score. The higher the activation, the more completely the column is filled by tokens the score calls base64, which is what the authors mean by calling the feature "quite specific" — though they give no percentage here, and the score itself is admittedly too broad.
  - fig-27 | The Base64 Feature Found Again in Model B: A separately trained transformer, decomposed by a separately trained dictionary, yields a feature (B/1/2165) whose activations correlate 0.85 with the base64 feature from the first model, and which fires almost exclusively on base64-looking strings at high activation. The paper offers this as evidence that the base64 feature is a property of models trained on this data rather than an artifact of one dictionary learning run.
  - fig-28 | B/1/2165's Base64 Logit Mode: In the second model's dictionary, the feature that matches the base64 feature does the same thing to the output that the original does: its logit-weight histogram has a small second mode, centered near +0.32, made almost entirely of tokens that would continue a base64 string. This is the downstream half of the universality claim — the two features do not just fire on the same text, they push up the same vocabulary.
  - fig-30 | The Base64 Feature's Nearest Neuron: The MLP neuron whose activations track the base64 feature most closely, A/neurons/470, correlates with it at only 0.18, and its own activation distribution stays mostly blue — mostly non-base64 — at every activation level. Its sampled examples are code, XML namespaces, pandoc `label="…"` markup, URL fragments and citation keys, categories with nothing in common but a punctuation-heavy texture. The paper reads this as the feature not being a neuron in disguise.
  - fig-32 | Base64 Feature Against Neuron 470: The neuron whose activations most resemble the base64 feature's shares only part of its behaviour: an activation correlation of 0.18 and a logit-weight correlation of 0.64, with most of the mass in both plots sitting off the diagonal. The paper reads this as consistent with the neuron helping represent the feature while largely doing other things, which is what it needs in order to say dictionary learning did not simply hand back an already-monosemantic base64 neuron.
  - fig-33 | The Hebrew Feature's Activation Spectrum: A histogram of how often feature A/1/416 takes each activation value, with each slice split by an independent Unicode-based score for "is this Hebrew". Strong activations are almost entirely Hebrew, which is the paper's specificity claim for this feature. The blue that survives at strong activations is mostly shared punctuation and whitespace, which the proxy scores as non-Hebrew by construction, so the paper reads it as a fault in the proxy rather than in the feature.
  - fig-37 | B/1/1901's Hebrew Logit-Weight Mode: For the Hebrew feature found in the second transformer's dictionary (B/1/1901), the distribution of logit weights over the vocabulary has the same shape reported for the first transformer's Hebrew feature: one large mode at zero, plus a small mode far to the right made of Hebrew-script tokens and the byte \xd7 that begins most Hebrew UTF-8 characters. The paper offers this as evidence that the matched features in the two runs do the same job downstream, not just fire at the same times.
  - fig-38 | Hebrew Feature Across Two Models: The Hebrew feature from run A has a counterpart in a second, independently trained run that fires on nearly the same tokens (activation correlation 0.92) but whose effects on next-token logits agree far less (0.3). The paper reads that gap the way it read the same gap for the Arabic feature: the tokens the feature is actually about agree closely, while a large central cloud of near-zero logit weights, which the paper guesses is unwanted interference, drags the overall correlation down.
  - eq-proxy-loglikelihood-ratio | The Log-Likelihood-Ratio Proxy: The paper scores every string with a hand-built number: how much more likely that string is under a guessed context (base64, Arabic script, DNA) than under the data as a whole. Because the score is computed from Unicode blocks and character counts, and never from the autoencoder, it gives an outside yardstick for checking whether a feature's proposed interpretation holds.
  - eq-bayes-script-proxy | Inverting the Script Probability: To score how Arabic-like a string is, the paper needs $P(s|\text{Arabic})$, which would otherwise require a generative model of Arabic text. Bayes' rule replaces it with three quantities that are cheap to compute: a character-level script check, a unigram probability, and the corpus rate of Arabic script. The same construction is reused for Hebrew and any other script with its own Unicode blocks.

NAME: The model and the autoencoder

THEME SUMMARY (shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat verbatim):
Two models are involved, and keeping them apart matters for everything that follows. One is the subject: a one-layer transformer with a 512-neuron MLP, overtrained on the Pile, whose MLP activations are the thing being decomposed. The other is the tool: a sparse autoencoder, wider than the layer it reads, trained to rebuild those activations from a small number of active hidden units, with an L1 penalty doing the work of forcing most units to zero. This theme also covers the training details the paper singles out as mattering — the tied pre-encoder bias, resampling of dead neurons — and the notation used to name a feature.

EVIDENCE ITEMS RELATED TO THIS THEME (link as [[item-id]]):
  - fig-1 | The UMAP Feature Browser: A screenshot of the interactive map the authors publish alongside the article: every dictionary direction from two autoencoder runs — 4,608 of them — projected onto a plane, with a searchable list of human-written feature labels beside it. It establishes that the qualitative families of features the paper describes in prose are also tight geometric clumps, and that features from the small run and the large run land in the same clumps. It is a browsing tool, not a measurement: the clusters carry the meaning, the coordinates do not.
  - fig-2 | Where the Dictionary Attaches: This schematic names the exact tensor the paper decomposes: the 512-dimensional post-ReLU MLP activation vector at a single token position of a one-layer transformer. It shows that vector being sent out to a sparse, overcomplete autoencoder and coming back, and that the thing the autoencoder returns is a set of between 512 and 131,072 feature activations rather than the 512 neuron values.
  - table-1 | The Two Models, Side by Side: The table is the paper's specification sheet for both models it works with: the small transformer under study on the left, and the sparse autoencoder trained on that transformer's MLP activations on the right. It establishes that the transformer is an ordinary language model trained in the ordinary way, and that the only unusual thing about the autoencoder is its width and its sparsity penalty. The paper flags that although it trains dictionaries up to 256 times wider than the MLP, the analyses it presents mostly use an 8x expansion.
  - fig-3 | The Simulated Larger Network: The figure states the paper's working picture of superposition: the network we can actually inspect is a squeezed-down projection of a much larger, much sparser network whose neurons would each be one clean feature. Because that projection packs more feature directions than there are neurons, the directions can only be almost orthogonal rather than orthogonal, and a single real neuron ends up sitting on several feature directions at once — which is what polysemanticity looks like from the neuron's point of view.
  - fig-4 | Anatomy of a Feature Page: A labelled tour of one page of the paper's published feature browser, which puts four kinds of evidence about a single learned feature side by side: where it fires, what it does to the next-token logits, whether any single neuron matches it, and whether a parallel training run found the same thing. For the feature shown, no neuron correlates with it above 0.09, while a feature from the parallel run correlates at 0.75.
  - fig-5 | The Feature Search Bar: A screenshot of the search strip in the paper's public feature browser, with its four dropdowns opened. It carries no results, so what it establishes is only the shape of the query the authors built for themselves: pick a matching mode, pick which part of a feature's record the query is matched against, pick a sort key over all features in a run, and pick which per-token loss difference is drawn as underlines.
  - fig-8 | The Arabic Feature's Activation Spectrum: The paper's specificity claim for the Arabic feature is not one number but a curve: the higher the feature fires, the more purely Arabic-script the text is. Barely-active tokens are roughly 25% Arabic, which is noisy, while tokens above activation 5 are about 98% Arabic. Since large activations move the model's logits most, the paper argues it is the clean high end that matters.
  - fig-9 | Arabic Feature's Activation Mass: Re-weighting the feature's activation histogram by activation magnitude shows that almost all of the total activation the Arabic script feature contributes comes from tokens the proxy scores as Arabic. The weak, less pure activations that dominate the raw token counts contribute almost nothing once weighted, which is the paper's case that the feature is specific in the way that matters.
  - fig-12 | Pinning the Arabic Script Feature: Holding one learned feature's activation at its maximum, with nothing else in the network touched, turns the model's continuation of a counting prefix into Arabic script. The paper offers this as its most direct causal check that the feature is used by the model to produce Arabic text, rather than merely co-occurring with it in the data.
  - fig-13 | The Arabic Feature in the Neuron Basis: The Arabic script feature's direction, written out one MLP neuron at a time, is spread across many neurons rather than living in one: 27 of the 512 coefficients have magnitude at least 0.1, and the three largest are negative. The paper reads this as evidence that dictionary learning did not simply rediscover an already-clean neuron.
  - fig-16 | The Arabic Feature Against Its Closest Neuron: Plotted against the single neuron whose behaviour is closest to it, the Arabic script feature separates Arabic contexts and Arabic output tokens along its own axis, while the neuron's axis does not separate them at all. The paper reads this as evidence that dictionary learning did not simply rediscover an already-clean neuron: correlations of 0.09 on activations and 0.21 on logit weights are non-zero but slight.

  - fig-45 | One Region, More Arrows: A hand-drawn schematic of what the paper means by feature splitting: widening the autoencoder does not send new features off to new parts of activation space, it packs more dictionary directions into the same narrow cone, so the split features end up at small angles to one another.   - fig-47 | The P Feature Zoo: One coarse feature that looked like a pointless "fires on the letter P" detector turns out, in wider dictionaries, to be a family of sixteen or more features that each fire on P in a different context — surnames, acronym patterns, biochemistry, LaTeX, legal citations. The paper offers this as its explanation for the strange single-token features that show up in small dictionaries: they are contexts the small dictionary could not tell apart, so widening the dictionary refines them rather than fragmenting them.

  - fig-49 | The Digit Feature That Won't Predict Digits: Two base64 features from the same dictionary push almost the same tokens up, except that the feature firing on single digits gives the ten digit tokens far less of a boost than its letter-firing sibling does. The paper reads this not as a broken feature but as the tokenizer showing through: byte-pair encoding merges adjacent digits, so a lone digit token is evidence the next token is not a lone digit.

  - fig-51 | Activation Similarity vs Logit Weight Similarity: The figure sets out the paper's two model-independent ways of asking whether the same feature was found twice, and shows that for the Arabic script feature they disagree: activation correlation between A/1/3450 and B/1/1334 is 0.91, logit weight correlation only 0.23.   - fig-52 | Feature vs Neuron Match Histograms: When you look for each item's closest counterpart in a second, independently trained one-layer transformer, autoencoder features match far better than raw MLP neurons: the typical feature's best match has activation correlation 0.72, the typical neuron's only 0.46.   - fig-57 | Unicode Prefix–Suffix Feature Loops: Where a script's characters are tokenized as a prefix byte plus a suffix byte, the paper finds a pair of features that hand off to each other: each one's top predicted tokens are exactly the tokens the other one fires on.   - fig-58 | The Four-Node HTML Automaton: Four features in the smallest dictionary hand off to each other through the token stream — open bracket, tag name, closing bracket, newline-and-indent, back to open bracket — so that together they behave like a small state machine that emits well-formed HTML such as `<div>\n\t\t<span>`. The paper stresses that nothing tied these four together during training: each learned its own job, and the loop exists because HTML in the training data has that shape.
  - fig-59 | The IRC Transcript Automaton: Four features from run A/0 hand off to each other across an IRC transcript: one fires on the "<" that opens a nickname, one on the nickname's interior, one on the closing ">", and one across the message text that follows.   - fig-63 | Decoder Sparsity in the Neuron Basis: In an outside replication, 92% of the learned feature directions turn out to be spread across many MLP neurons rather than concentrated in one neuron or ten.   - fig-65 | Overlapping Density Clusters in Run A/5: The paper's quick diagnostic — read two separated humps off a log-scale histogram of how often each feature fires — stops working at the largest autoencoder sizes. In run A/5, with 131,072 hidden units, the ultralow-density features run continuously into the rest of the distribution instead of sitting apart, so density alone no longer tells the uninterpretable leftovers from the real features. The paper reports this as a pattern it has seen, not as a measured effect.
  - fig-66 | Three Diagnostics That Isolate the High-Density Cluster: In a run where feature density alone leaves the two modes overlapping, plotting each feature's density together with its encoder bias and its decoder-encoder dot product pulls the high-density cluster out as a compact, separate blob. That separation is what lets the paper draw a line around the ultralow-density features and set them aside as a training artifact rather than as things the transformer actually computes.
  - fig-67 | Density Histograms Across Dictionary Sizes: Across five sparsity settings, each panel overlays the feature-density histograms of six autoencoders whose dictionaries run from 512 up to 131,072 units. Widening the dictionary does not just yield more features: it keeps pushing the left edge of the distribution further left, so rarer features keep appearing. The paper reads this as a sign that no dictionary size tried so far has exhausted the one-layer transformer's features, and states it as a suggestion rather than a result.
  - fig-69 | Pearson vs Spearman Scoring Panel: The paper re-runs its automated interpretability scoring with Pearson correlation alongside the Spearman correlation used throughout the main text, and the two give visibly the same distributions and the same ordering: dictionary-learning features score well above transformer neurons, while features from a randomized model sit awkwardly high. This is a check that the headline gap does not depend on which correlation coefficient was chosen, not a new measurement.
  - fig-72 | Explanation Length Distributions: A prompting bug sometimes let Claude's whole chain of thought stand in as a feature's explanation, producing explanations several hundred tokens long instead of a few dozen. This figure shows that the long tail appears in all three scored populations at similar rates — 19% for neurons, 15% for the randomized model, 13% for the A/1 features — and the appendix reports that splitting scores into long-explanation and short-explanation groups does not change them significantly. It is a caveat check the authors ran, not a result about features.
  - eq-feature-decomposition | Activations as a Sum of Features: The paper defines a decomposition of a layer as writing each token's MLP activation vector as a fixed bias plus a weighted sum of unit directions, one direction per feature. The weights are the feature activations, and the sum runs over many more features than the vector has dimensions, which the paper says is only workable because the weights are sparse.
  - eq-encoder | The Encoder Equation: This fixes what a "feature activation" concretely is in this paper: one non-negative number per dictionary element, produced by a single linear readout of the MLP activation vector followed by ReLU, computed after a learned offset has been subtracted from the input. Everything the paper later says about a feature firing, weakly or strongly, is a statement about this quantity.
  - eq-autoencoder-loss | The Sparse Autoencoder's Two-Term Loss: This is the entire method in four lines: a ReLU encoder turns one MLP activation vector into feature activations, a linear decoder rebuilds the activation from them, and training minimizes squared reconstruction error plus an L1 penalty on those activations. The two loss terms pull against each other — reconstruction forces the dictionary to keep the information, L1 forces most features to be off — and the coefficient lambda sets the exchange rate between them.
  - eq-feature-ablation | Subtracting One Feature's Contribution: Ablating a feature means subtracting exactly that feature's own contribution — its activation times its dictionary direction — from the MLP activation at every token, then finishing the forward pass. Because everything else in the activation is left alone, the resulting change in the model's predicted log-likelihood is evidence that the model actually uses the feature, not merely that the feature co-occurs with the text.

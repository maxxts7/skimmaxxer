NAME: Per-feature dictionary diagnostic scatter

SUMMARY (already shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat it verbatim):
A scatter in which every point is one learned feature, placed by numbers read off its own encoder and decoder vectors — how concentrated its direction is in the neuron basis, its bias, its decoder-encoder dot product, its density. It is for claims about a whole dictionary at once, made before anyone has interpreted a single feature.

DRAFT EXPLANATION from the extraction stage (your page replaces this; keep what is right, go deeper):
### Why this chart and not another

Both figures make a claim about a whole dictionary at once, and both have to make it without knowing what any feature means. The only inputs are the autoencoder's own weights: per feature, one column of the [[dictionary-decoder]] and one row of the encoder. No tokens, no activations, no human reading. The numbers can be computed for every feature in a run, which is what the claims need: they are about the population, not about examples.

A summary number was available in each case and would have hidden the point. In the replication, the reported split — 4% of features dominated by a single neuron, 4% by their top ten, 92% neither — is the table version. The scatter shows those labels are threshold cuts through one continuous cloud, not three kinds of thing. In the density appendix an average is the wrong instrument: the claim is that features fall into two groups, and a mean sits between them. Two axes would not do either — the third diagnostic is added because no single one of the three pulls the groups apart alone.

### How to read it, step by step

One point is one feature, meaning one hidden unit of the [[sparse-autoencoder]], placed by numbers derived from its own weights.

In the decoder-sparsity version, square every entry of the feature's decoder column and divide by their total. The x-axis is the largest single share; the y-axis is shares two through ten added up. Both are fractions of the same total, so $x + y \le 1$, and no one of the next nine can beat the top one, so $y \le 9x$ — the straight diagonal edge cutting the upper left, above which nothing can sit. Colour is a label the author assigned by thresholding those shares (red 1-sparse, green 10-sparse, blue dense); the cutoffs are not printed, so colour is his reading drawn onto the plot rather than a separate measurement. Look at the marginal histograms first: the scatter overplots hardest where most features are.

In the three-diagnostic version, depth is [[feature-density]] on a $\log_{10}$ scale from $-10$ to $-3$, logarithmic because densities span that many orders of magnitude. Height is the feature's entry of the encoder bias $\mathbf{b}_e$ in $\mathbf{f} = \mathrm{ReLU}(W_e\bar{\mathbf{x}} + \mathbf{b}_e)$ ([[autoencoder-encoder]]), always negative and readable as a firing threshold. The horizontal axis is the dot product of the feature's decoder column with its encoder row, which carries information only because the two are learned separately ([[untied-decoder-weights]]). Colour re-encodes depth. Look for the shape of the cloud, not for points: whether one compact blob stands clear of the large mass.

### What a bad result would look like

If learned features were mostly neurons wearing new names, the decoder-sparsity cloud would pile against the right edge, $x$ near 1 and $y$ near 0, with the top marginal spiking there. Instead the blue mass sits below $x = 0.2$ — the bulk form of what the case studies find one feature at a time ([[neuron-basis-coefficients]], [[features-invisible-in-the-neuron-basis]]), and what [[superposition]] predicts. The plot does not display the [[kurtosis-privileged-basis-test]] claim attached to it, that the [[privileged-basis]] survives anyway; that one comes from the text.

For the three-diagnostic version, failure is a gradient instead of an island — orange shading continuously into crimson with no gap, which is what density alone gives in large runs with a large [[l1-coefficient]] ([[feature-density-histogram]]). A single rendered angle can also fool you: a 3-D projection may open a gap that closes on rotation, and the reader gets one view. And separation, however clean, does not show the [[ultralow-density-cluster]] is a training artifact ([[density-cluster-separation]]); it supplies a boundary to draw, no more.

### Where it comes back

Twice, in different hands. Figure 63 sits in the [[external-replication]] section: two axes plus marginals, categorical colour, an outside researcher's dictionary, asking whether features are neurons. Figure 66 sits in the feature-density appendix: three axes, continuous colour, the authors' own run, asking whether the [[high-density-cluster]] can be fenced off — its darkest points, at the sparse extreme, being [[de-facto-dead-neurons]]. The reading habit holds across both: points are features, coordinates come from weights alone, and the evidence is where the cloud sits, never where a point sits.

THEME IT BELONGS TO: theme-how-to-read-the-charts | How to read the charts - This paper argues with a small number of chart shapes, used over and over with different data poured into them. Each one is explained once here: why that shape was chosen, how to read it, and what it would look like if the claim it supports were false. The order follows where a reader meets them — schematics and browser screenshots first, then the per-feature evidence, then the population plots, then the geometry and the training diagnostics. These teach how to read the evidence rather than what it says, so they sit apart from the argument, at the end.

PREREQUISITES:
  - sparse-autoencoder | Sparse autoencoder: A one-hidden-layer autoencoder with a wide ReLU hidden layer and an L1 penalty on that layer, trained to reconstruct MLP activations. Its hidden units are the learned features and its decoder columns are the feature directions.
  - dictionary-decoder | Decoder as Dictionary: The output weight matrix $W_d$, whose columns are the feature directions. The paper uses "decoder" and "dictionary" interchangeably.
  - autoencoder-encoder | Encoder: The linear map plus bias plus ReLU that turns an activation vector into feature activations: $\mathbf{f} = \text{ReLU}(W_e\bar{\mathbf{x}} + \mathbf{b}_e)$.
  - feature-direction | Feature direction: The unit vector $\mathbf{d}_i$ in activation space along which feature $i$ writes when it fires; in the autoencoder these are the columns of the decoder weight matrix.
  - feature-density | Feature Density: The fraction of tokens on which a feature has a nonzero value; the paper's main quick proxy for autoencoder quality.
  - superposition | Superposition: A hypothesized phenomenon in which a network represents more independent features than it has neurons, by giving each feature its own linear combination of neurons.
  - privileged-basis | Privileged basis: A basis that the architecture singles out — here the neuron basis, made special by the elementwise activation function — so that features have some tendency to align with it even though they need not.
  - untied-decoder-weights | Untied Decoder Weights: The encoder weights are learned independently of the decoder weights rather than being constrained to their transpose. The paper reports the two end up meaningfully different.

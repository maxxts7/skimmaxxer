NAME: Hebrew Feature (A/1/416)

SUMMARY (already shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat it verbatim):
A feature firing on Hebrew script, with high specificity in the upper activation spectrum and weak activations on other Unicode-script languages.

DRAFT EXPLANATION from the extraction stage (your page replaces this; keep what is right, go deeper):
A/1/416 has high activation specificity in the upper part of its spectrum, but weakly activates for other things, especially other languages written in non-Latin Unicode scripts. Some of the low-proxy activity at strong activations lands on characters shared across scripts, such as whitespace and punctuation. Its logit weights have a second mode of Hebrew characters and partial-codepoint tokens, notably \xd7, which begins the UTF-8 encoding of most characters in the basic Hebrew Unicode block. The proxy correlation is 0.55; the paper suggests part of the sensitivity shortfall is absorbed by A/1/1016. No neuron matches it: the most correlated neuron, A/neurons/489, reaches only 0.1, and a search for neurons with the main Hebrew Unicode block in their top dataset examples found none.

THEME IT BELONGS TO: theme-four-features-up-close | Four features up close - Having a decomposition is not the same as having interpretable features, so the paper picks four — Arabic script, DNA, base64, Hebrew — and tries to establish the same five claims about each. The claims are that the feature fires only in its context, fires whenever that context is present, changes the model's output in a matching way, is not just a neuron in disguise, and turns up again in a separately trained model. The specificity argument leans on a hand-built proxy: a cheap score for whether a string looks like the hypothesized context, computed without reference to the model. The paper says outright that these four were chosen for being easy to analyze, not for being typical, which is why the next theme exists.

The paper reads the low 0–1 activation band as slightly cleaner here than in the original feature, which is an eyeball comparison rather than a measured one.
  - fig-33 | The Hebrew Feature's Activation Spectrum: A histogram of how often feature A/1/416 takes each activation value, with each slice split by an independent Unicode-based score for "is this Hebrew". Strong activations are almost entirely Hebrew, which is the paper's specificity claim for this feature. The blue that survives at strong activations is mostly shared punctuation and whitespace, which the proxy scores as non-Hebrew by construction, so the paper reads it as a fault in the proxy rather than in the feature.
  - fig-34 | Hebrew Feature Logit Weights: The Hebrew feature's effect on the model's output is concentrated in a small second bump far to the right of the main mode, and the tokens in that bump are Hebrew letters plus the byte `\xd7`. Because `\xd7` is the shared first byte of the UTF-8 encoding of most basic-Hebrew characters, pushing up that one token amounts to pushing up the whole script — evidence that the feature does not merely co-occur with Hebrew but acts to produce it.
  - fig-35 | The Hebrew Feature Against Its Closest Neuron: The neuron whose activations track the Hebrew feature most closely does not behave like the feature: the correlation is 0.1 on activations and 0.38 on logit weights, and in both plots the feature's axis cleanly separates Hebrew while the neuron's axis does not. The paper takes this as evidence that dictionary learning did not simply hand back an already-Hebrew neuron under a new name.
  - fig-36 | Hebrew Feature Found Again in Model B: A dictionary trained on a second, independently seeded transformer contains a feature, B/1/1901, whose activation histogram looks like the Hebrew feature's: the strong end of its firing range is dominated by tokens a Unicode-based Hebrew score calls Hebrew, and its activations correlate 0.92 with A/1/416's across a large token sample. The paper reads this as evidence that the Hebrew feature is not an accident of one training run, though it rests on one matched pair and one correlation number.
  - fig-37 | B/1/1901's Hebrew Logit-Weight Mode: For the Hebrew feature found in the second transformer's dictionary (B/1/1901), the distribution of logit weights over the vocabulary has the same shape reported for the first transformer's Hebrew feature: one large mode at zero, plus a small mode far to the right made of Hebrew-script tokens and the byte \xd7 that begins most Hebrew UTF-8 characters. The paper offers this as evidence that the matched features in the two runs do the same job downstream, not just fire at the same times.
  - fig-38 | Hebrew Feature Across Two Models: The Hebrew feature from run A has a counterpart in a second, independently trained run that fires on nearly the same tokens (activation correlation 0.92) but whose effects on next-token logits agree far less (0.3). The paper reads that gap the way it read the same gap for the Arabic feature: the tokens the feature is actually about agree closely, while a large central cloud of near-zero logit weights, which the paper guesses is unwanted interference, drags the overall correlation down.
  - fig-43 | Pinning Features to Steer Generation: Holding a single learned feature at a high value while sampling makes the model emit text of exactly the kind that feature normally fires on — Arabic script, DNA, base64, hexadecimal, Hebrew, Han Chinese, or uppercase. Because the autoencoder was fitted only to MLP activations and never to the model's outputs, the paper reads this as evidence that these features are used by the model's computation, not just artifacts of correlations in the data.

### Why this chart and not another

The paper cannot argue its case one number at a time. Its per-feature argument is a fixed set of claims — the [[feature-evidence-template|five-claim template]] — and each claim comes in a different unit: a purity fraction, a next-token [[logit-weight]], a Pearson correlation against neurons, a firing rate. There is no shared axis, so there is no single plot. The browser lays the four kinds of evidence side by side on one screen and lets a reader see whether they agree about the same [[feature]]. A table of scores would have hidden the disagreements — a feature can look clean on its top examples and messy three bands down, and the layout keeps both in view.

The second reason is scale. Run A/1 has 4,096 features and the paper discusses four in depth. A bar chart of average interpretability would let the authors choose what to average and would inherit [[max-dataset-example-bias|the maximal-example problem]]: judge any unit by its strongest firings and it looks monosemantic. Publishing the browser hands the check to the reader, who can open a feature the paper never mentions. The search strip is the other half of that. It carries no results at all; what it establishes is the shape of query the authors could put to their own [[dictionary-learning-run|run]].

### How to read it, step by step

Everything outside the grey panel is annotation added for the paper. The [[feature-exploration-interface|interface]] prints none of it.

A feature page reads in four columns. First, identity and three comparison tables: NEURON ALIGNMENT decomposes the [[feature-direction]] into the 512 MLP neurons ([[neuron-basis-coefficients]]); CORRELATED NEURONS ranks neurons by how well their activations track the feature's ([[most-correlated-neuron]]); CORRELATED B FEATURES runs the same comparison against a dictionary trained on [[transformer-a-and-b|a second transformer differing only in seed]], which is [[universality]] measured by [[activation-similarity]].

Second column, two histograms. The upper one has [[feature-activation]] on $x$ — a ReLU output, so non-negative — and counts of sampled tokens on $y$, with exact zeros dropped and the firing rate quoted separately as [[feature-density]]. The lower one plots logit weights over the whole vocabulary, centred because softmax is shift-invariant, with ten tokens from each tail listed above it.

Third column, the [[top-dataset-examples|top 20 activating examples]]. Fourth, [[activation-interval|subsample intervals]] stepping down from the maximum, which is where an interpretation tends to fail.

Colour carries two channels at once. Orange background is activation strength on that token; underline colour is [[feature-ablation|ablation effect]] — blue where deleting the feature raised the loss, red where it lowered it ([[ablation-effect-visualization]]). Look at CORRELATED NEURONS first, then the intervals — not the top examples.

The search strip reads left to right as one query: a match mode, the field matched against (examples, logits, index, or the [[automated-interpretability|machine-written explanation]]), a sort key over all features, and Underline Mode, which chooses whether the underlines mean one feature ablated or the whole MLP replaced by the reconstruction ([[loss-recovered-by-features]]).

### What a bad result would look like

On the page shown, the strongest correlated neuron reaches only $+0.09$ while the best matching B-run feature reaches $+0.75$. Flip either and the page collapses. A correlated neuron at $0.9$ would mean [[dictionary-learning]] handed back a neuron under a new name. A best B feature at $0.1$ would mean the feature is an artifact of one seed. Coherent top examples above interval bands that read as noise would mean the interpretation holds only at the top of the range. A logit histogram with no tails would mean the feature does nothing downstream.

The page does not hide its weak spots: the human label reads `Latin: ?`, and the automated score is $0.305$ ([[activation-prediction-scoring]]) — above zero, far from a match.

### Where it comes back

As a whole shape, only twice: fig-4 and fig-5. Its panels return separately — activation histograms, logit tails, neuron-correlation tables, shaded text — through the case studies of Section 3. What changes between the two figures is that one carries evidence and the other carries none; what changes between browser pages is the run, so the same layout on a wider dictionary shows [[feature-splitting]] instead of one feature.

THEME IT BELONGS TO: theme-how-to-read-the-charts | How to read the charts - This paper argues with a small number of chart shapes, used over and over with different data poured into them. Each one is explained once here: why that shape was chosen, how to read it, and what it would look like if the claim it supports were false. The order follows where a reader meets them — schematics and browser screenshots first, then the per-feature evidence, then the population plots, then the geometry and the training diagnostics. These teach how to read the evidence rather than what it says, so they sit apart from the argument, at the end.

PREREQUISITES:
  - feature | Feature: A basic interpretable direction in activation space — the unit the paper wants to decompose models into, in place of neurons.
  - sparse-autoencoder | Sparse autoencoder: A one-hidden-layer autoencoder with a wide ReLU hidden layer and an L1 penalty on that layer, trained to reconstruct MLP activations. Its hidden units are the learned features and its decoder columns are the feature directions.
  - dictionary-learning | Dictionary learning: The problem of writing each activation vector as a sparse combination of directions drawn from a fixed, larger-than-necessary set. The paper frames the search for a model's features as exactly this problem.
  - superposition | Superposition: A hypothesized phenomenon in which a network represents more independent features than it has neurons, by giving each feature its own linear combination of neurons.
  - polysemanticity | Polysemanticity: A single neuron responding to a mixture of seemingly unrelated inputs, which makes it a poor unit for human understanding of the network.
  - feature-activation | Feature activation: The scalar $f_i(\mathbf{x})$ giving how strongly feature $i$ fires on a datapoint; in the autoencoder it is the ReLU hidden-layer value.
  - feature-direction | Feature direction: The unit vector $\mathbf{d}_i$ in activation space along which feature $i$ writes when it fires; in the autoencoder these are the columns of the decoder weight matrix.
  - feature-evidence-template | The Five-Claim Feature Template: The fixed set of five claims the paper tries to establish for each individual feature it studies in depth: specificity, sensitivity, downstream effect, non-correspondence to a neuron, and universality.
  - feature-notation | Feature Notation: The paper's identifier scheme: run seed, autoencoder size index, and feature index, as in A/1/3450 or B/1/1334; neurons are written A/neurons/489.

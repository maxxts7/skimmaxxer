NAME: Features-versus-neurons score histogram

SUMMARY (already shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat it verbatim):
Two populations of units — the autoencoder's learned features and the transformer's own MLP neurons — scored on one number and drawn as overlapping histograms on a shared axis. It is how the paper argues at population scale, rather than by example, that its features are more interpretable and more reproducible than neurons.

DRAFT EXPLANATION from the extraction stage (your page replaces this; keep what is right, go deeper):
### Why this chart and not another

The paper's case is comparative: it claims the directions its [[sparse-autoencoder|sparse autoencoder]] learns are more interpretable than the MLP neurons already in the model. Elsewhere it argues that one feature at a time, which invites the charge of [[cherry-picked-case-studies|cherry-picking]]. The histogram is the same claim at population scale — every scored unit is in the picture.

Given one number per unit, a mean or a two-row table is the obvious move, and it would hide what the paper most wants shown. In the hand-scored figure neurons average 3.2 but their *median* is 0: an average suggests a mediocre middle, when most neurons scored nothing at all and a thin minority scored well. A distribution also shows whether the gap is a whole population shifted or a tail doing the work. Overlaying both on one axis makes separation, or its absence, the visible fact.

### How to read it, step by step

The horizontal axis is one score, computed once per unit, and a "unit" is either one autoencoder [[feature]] (one dictionary direction) or one MLP neuron of the [[one-layer-transformer-testbed|one-layer model]]. Which score changes by figure: a 0–14 rubric total, a [[spearman-correlation]] between predicted and true activations, a yes/no accuracy, a [[pearson-correlation]] to the nearest match in a second model.

The vertical axis is usually **density**, not count: each population is separately normalised to enclose area 1. The populations are unequal — about 3,676 features against 512 neurons — so raw counts would erase the neurons. It also means bar height says nothing about how many units were scored. To convert, multiply height by bin width: height $4$ over bins of width $0.02$ is about $8\%$ of that population. Only the hand-scored figure uses Count, where the samples are small and similar in size.

Crimson is features, teal is neurons, yellow where a third population (the [[randomized-transformer]] baseline) appears. Dark navy is the two translucent fills lying on top of each other — overlap, not a third group. That is the trap the chart sets, and navy area is exactly the evidence *against* the claim.

Look first at the two small triangles under the axis: those are the medians, and the distance between them is the claim. Then the dotted "random chance" line, if there is one, and whether the teal mode sits on it. Then the legend chips, which repeat median and mean and give a "failures" count — runs where the scoring model replied in a format the harness could not parse, so the unit was dropped rather than scored badly. Those describe the pipeline, not the units. Never read a claim off one bar.

### What a bad result would look like

Two histograms lying on each other: mostly navy, one shared mode, the triangles nearly touching. That would say the learned directions are no easier to explain than the neurons the model came with — that [[dictionary-learning]] bought nothing. Weaker failures count too: crimson beating teal on the mean while both share a mode, so the typical feature is no better and a tail carries the average; or teal already well above chance, undercutting the [[polysemanticity|polysemantic-neuron]] premise that motivated the approach. The importance-weighted figure comes close to the first bad picture, and the paper says so rather than burying it.

### Where it comes back

Fig-39 is the human arm: a [[feature-interpretability-rubric|rubric]] total from a [[blinded-annotation|blinded]] rater, counts rather than density, medians 12 against 0. Fig-40 and fig-41 are the two [[automated-interpretability]] arms — activation prediction (0.58 against 0.2, with a teal spike at exactly zero that is a scoring artifact) and [[automated-logit-scoring|logit-weight prediction]] against a 0.5 chance line (0.73 against 0.53). Fig-52 reuses the shape for a different question, [[universality]]: the axis is agreement with the closest match in an independently trained model, medians 0.72 against 0.46, with example cards pinned to points on the axis. Fig-71 replots fig-40's score after [[importance-scoring]] reweights the sampling, and both populations slump toward zero. Fig-72 is the odd one out: three populations in stacked rows rather than overlaid, and its axis is explanation length, not a score — a caveat check whose good outcome is three rows that look alike.

PREREQUISITES:
  - feature | Feature: A basic interpretable direction in activation space — the unit the paper wants to decompose models into, in place of neurons.
  - sparse-autoencoder | Sparse autoencoder: A one-hidden-layer autoencoder with a wide ReLU hidden layer and an L1 penalty on that layer, trained to reconstruct MLP activations. Its hidden units are the learned features and its decoder columns are the feature directions.
  - dictionary-learning | Dictionary learning: The problem of writing each activation vector as a sparse combination of directions drawn from a fixed, larger-than-necessary set. The paper frames the search for a model's features as exactly this problem.
  - polysemanticity | Polysemanticity: A single neuron responding to a mixture of seemingly unrelated inputs, which makes it a poor unit for human understanding of the network.
  - superposition | Superposition: A hypothesized phenomenon in which a network represents more independent features than it has neurons, by giving each feature its own linear combination of neurons.
  - one-layer-transformer-testbed | The One-Layer Transformer Testbed: The model under study: a one-layer transformer with a 128-dimensional residual stream and a 512-neuron ReLU MLP, overtrained on 100 billion Pile tokens.
  - neuron-notation | Neuron Notation: "A/neurons/32" names a transformer MLP neuron rather than a learned feature, so the two can be compared without ambiguity.
  - spearman-correlation | Spearman Rank Correlation: Correlation computed on the ranks of values rather than the values themselves.
  - pearson-correlation | Pearson Correlation: The standard linear correlation coefficient between two variables, ranging from -1 to 1.

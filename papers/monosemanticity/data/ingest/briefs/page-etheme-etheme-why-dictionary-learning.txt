NAME: Why dictionary learning was the option left

THEME SUMMARY (shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat verbatim):
The paper starts from superposition: a layer with 512 neurons may carry many more features by giving each its own direction. More directions than dimensions is overcompleteness, and directions that outnumber dimensions cannot all be orthogonal, so switching one feature on slightly switches others on — interference. That picture is also the reason the authors abandon the other route, which was building models that have no superposition to start with: training the subject model for activation sparsity does not deliver monosemanticity, and a four-feature calculation shows cross-entropy loss prefers a neuron that fires ambiguously on two things to one that fires cleanly on one. What is left is dictionary learning, and the paper picks a deliberately weak solver — a one-hidden-layer autoencoder with an L1 penalty, in place of the classical iterative algorithms it tried and set aside. The argument for weakness is that exact sparse recovery is NP-hard, so a method strong enough to succeed at it might be pulling structure out of the activations that the model itself has no way to read; the penalty is licensed by the older hypothesis that the variables generating natural data are mostly inactive at any moment.

  e001 [load-bearing] sparse-autoencoder (Sparse autoencoder) --depends-on--> sparsity-of-natural-latent-variables (Sparsity of Natural Latent Variables) | sparsity assumption licenses the penalty: The autoencoder is trained so that most of its hidden units are exactly zero on any given token. The paper does not derive that constraint from the transformer. It borrows a long-standing hypothesis that the latent variables behind natural data are mostly inactive at any moment, and asks for a sparse decomposition on that basis.
  e002 [load-bearing] overcompleteness (Overcompleteness) --depends-on--> superposition (Superposition) | more directions than dimensions, because superposition: The dictionary is deliberately made wider than the activation vector it decomposes. That only makes sense if you believe the model packs more features into the layer than it has neurons, which is what the superposition hypothesis claims. The paper insists overcompleteness is what separates its setting from disentanglement work, which looks for at most as many factors as there are dimensions.
  e003 [load-bearing] interference (Interference) --depends-on--> overcompleteness (Overcompleteness) | non-orthogonality forced by the extra directions: You cannot fit more than 512 mutually perpendicular directions into a 512-dimensional space. So once the feature set is overcomplete, some pairs of feature directions must have a nonzero dot product. Activating one feature then slightly activates its neighbours, and the paper calls that cross-talk interference.
  e004 [load-bearing] weak-dictionary-learning (A deliberately weak dictionary learning algorithm) --depends-on--> np-hardness-of-exact-sparse-recovery (NP-Hardness of Exact Recovery) | NP-hardness makes strong solvers suspect: Recovering the sparse feature vector from a lower-dimensional activation is compressed sensing, which in its exact form is NP-hard. The transformer is certainly not solving an NP-hard problem when it reads its own activations. The paper turns that into a worry: a sophisticated recovery method might dig out structure the model itself has no way to use.
  e005 [supporting] sparse-autoencoder (Sparse autoencoder) --depends-on--> weak-dictionary-learning (A deliberately weak dictionary learning algorithm) | weakness is why this method was picked: Having decided that a strong solver could over-recover, the paper wants a method about as capable as the model's own machinery. A one-hidden-layer ReLU autoencoder is close in architecture to a transformer MLP layer. That similarity, plus its ability to scale to the 8 billion activation vectors used here, is the stated reason classical dictionary learning algorithms were tried and set aside.
  e030 [load-bearing] noisy-simulation (Noisy simulation of a larger network) --depends-on--> feature-sparsity (Feature sparsity) | sparsity is what makes simulation cheap: Superposition is described as a small network approximately simulating a larger, sparser one. The simulation works because only a few features are active on any given token, so directions that are not orthogonal rarely fire at the same time. If many features fired at once the interference would be constant rather than occasional.
  e107 [load-bearing] architectural-approaches-to-superposition (Architectural approaches, and why they were abandoned) --contrasts-with--> dictionary-learning (Dictionary learning) | two of three strategies, one abandoned: Toy Models laid out building models that lack superposition and decomposing models that have it as separate routes. The authors say they spent significant time on the first and concluded it is not merely costly but fundamentally non-viable for language models. This paper is the second route, taken up because the first did not work.
  e108 [load-bearing] activation-sparsity-training (Inducing activation sparsity during training) --contrasts-with--> monosemanticity (Monosemanticity) | removing superposition does not buy interpretability: Training the subject model to have few active neurons, even to the point of one-hot activations, does eliminate superposition. It does not produce cleanly interpretable neurons. Individual neurons stay polysemantic, because representing several features ambiguously can reach a lower loss than representing one of them cleanly and ignoring the rest.
  e109 [load-bearing] cross-entropy-favors-polysemanticity (The four-feature loss calculation) --contrasts-with--> mse-loss-and-monosemanticity (Why MSE models differ from language models) | the loss function decides the answer: In the paper's four-feature toy calculation under cross-entropy, a single binary neuron firing ambiguously on features A and B gets loss ln 2, about 0.7, while firing cleanly on A alone gets (3/4)ln 3, about 0.8. So cross-entropy prefers the polysemantic neuron. Under mean squared error, prior work finds the two solutions can tie, and for some feature importance curves the monosemantic one wins. Language models are trained with cross-entropy, which is the authors' reason for giving up on architectural fixes.
  e110 [supporting] solu (SoLU activation) --contrasts-with--> monosemanticity (Monosemanticity) | some neurons cleaner, others worse: SoLU was an earlier attempt to make features line up with the neuron basis. The paper reports that it appears to make some neurons more interpretable at the cost of making others even less interpretable than before. The gain is not free, and no total accounting of the trade is offered here.
  e111 [supporting] disentanglement (Disentanglement) --contrasts-with--> overcompleteness (Overcompleteness) | as many factors as dimensions, or more: The disentanglement literature looks for a representation whose dimensions separate distinct influences on the data, typically as many factors as the space has dimensions. This paper asks for more directions than dimensions. It insists the difference is not cosmetic: overcompleteness is what turns the inner problem into compressed sensing rather than an ordinary matrix factorization.
  e112 [supporting] classical-dictionary-learning-algorithms (Classical Dictionary Learning Algorithms) --contrasts-with--> sparse-autoencoder (Sparse autoencoder) | established methods set aside for a simpler one: The authors tried classical iterative methods such as Method of Optimal Directions and K-SVD, and moved on. The stated reason is scale: the sparse autoencoder trains on 8 billion activation vectors, which they believe is necessary to find rare features. A second reason follows in the next link.
  e113 [supporting] weak-dictionary-learning (A deliberately weak dictionary learning algorithm) --contrasts-with--> np-hardness-of-exact-sparse-recovery (NP-Hardness of Exact Recovery) | a method too strong would prove nothing: Exact compressed sensing is NP-hard, so any method solving it exactly is doing something the transformer certainly is not doing. That is the paper's argument for deliberately preferring a weak decomposition method. A sparse autoencoder is architecturally close to an MLP layer, so structure it can recover is more plausibly structure the model itself can read.

### Why this chart and not another

The [[timestamp-reconstruction]] method has one rule for the inside of a gap: between two [[timestamp-anchors]], points where an agent saw the real clock, the elapsed time is split evenly across the transcript messages in between, one equal step per message ([[timestamp-interpolation]]). Figure 17 reports how well that rule did across 139,128 held-out anchors: a median error of 12 seconds and 99.8% within five minutes. The report then says those numbers describe only the places the [[timestamp-validation]] test could reach, which are the dense ones. The question left over is what the rule does where anchors are thin. A single number for the worst case, 550.75 minutes, would say how far it missed and nothing about why. A histogram of the tail would fold six different geometries into one bar. A table of estimated and observed times would hide the layout of anchors that produced the miss. Drawing each span, with its anchors and the line the rule drew through them, is the only form that shows the failure has one shape: an equal-step rule crossing a stretch where the steps were not equal.

### How to read it, step by step

Each of the six panels is one hidden anchor from one [[agent-transcript]], ranked by error, worst at top left. The horizontal axis is position in the transcript, counted in messages from "first action in span" to "last action in span"; it is not time. The vertical axis is clock time, rising upward, with no tick labels; the real times are printed under the panel as estimated and observed, in UTC. Solid blue dots are the anchors that were kept. The hollow blue circle is the hidden anchor at its true time. The green line runs straight, in time against message count, between the kept anchors on either side of the gap: that line is the production rule. The orange dot sits on the green line directly above or below the hollow circle and is the estimate. The dashed orange segment joining them is the error, and its length in minutes is the subheading. The footer gives the count: six largest of 139,128 eligible holdouts.

The slope of the green line is the first thing to read. Because the x-axis is message count, a steep line means much wall-clock time passed over few messages, which is a stretch where one or a few tool calls ran long. The rule cannot tell which message took the time, so it spreads it evenly. The hollow circle below the line means the estimate was too late (examples 1, 3, 4); above it, too early (examples 2, 5, 6). The hidden anchor's position comes last: mid-gap between distant anchors in examples 1 and 3, one step before the final anchor in example 2.

### What a bad result would look like

The version that would undermine the report is panels whose blue dots sit close together on both axes and whose dashed segment is long anyway. That would mean the rule fails where the report says it works, and every hour-by-hour figure built on these timestamps would be suspect. A second bad version is the same six geometries without the aggregate behind them: six hours-off cases are tolerable only because Figure 17 puts 99.8% of cases within five minutes. As drawn, every miss coincides with sparse anchors or a span end, which is what the caption claims. What the panels cannot show is the regions the test excluded, gaps with an anchor on only one side and the starts and ends of transcripts; the report says estimates there "may be off by hours" and does not measure it.

### Where it comes back

No other figure uses this shape. Figure 17 is its aggregate companion: the same test summed into a cumulative curve and threshold bars, plotted only to 6.5 minutes, so these six panels are the part of that curve off its right edge. The timestamps the panels test feed every clock-based figure in the report, including Figure 16, Figure 19 and the sequence of events in section 4.2, and the panels are the reason those figures note that timestamps are reconstructed and may have some error.

  - timestamp-anchors | Timestamp anchors: Points where an agent saw the real time, for example by running date or receiving a fresh HTTP response date, used as fixed reference times.
  - timestamp-interpolation | Interpolation by message count: Estimating times between two anchors by linear interpolation over the number of transcript messages between them, with extrapolation only near a good linear fit.
  - timestamp-validation | Leave-one-out validation (Figures 17-18): Testing accuracy by hiding one known anchor and predicting it from its neighbours; across ~139,000 cases the median error was about 12 seconds and 99.8% were within five minutes.
  - agent-transcript | Agent transcript: The record of a single agent run's actions and raw reasoning; both a primary data source for the investigation and the object agents tried to tamper with.

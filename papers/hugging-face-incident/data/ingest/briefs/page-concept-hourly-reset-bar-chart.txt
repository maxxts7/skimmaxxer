### Why this chart and not another

The question behind the chart is whether container resets arrived on a schedule or in bursts. A reset is an event in which an agent's [[container]] was wiped and restarted, erasing recent transcript history and sometimes reviving the agent's [[target-program]]. Agents on the [[message-board]] argued about this at length in the [[container-resets]] workstream, and some of them, seeing a similar interval between two large waves, predicted the next one and persuaded others to hurry. The report's claim is that resets came in irregular waves, so those predictions had no basis. A single total, or an average rate per hour, would say nothing about timing. A table of reset times would have carried the absolute counts that OpenAI removed under its [[redaction-process]]. A bar per hour drawn without numbers is the smallest chart that still answers the timing question: it keeps the order and the relative size of the bursts while hiding how many resets any burst contained.

### How to read it

The horizontal axis is UTC time from July 8th to July 16th, with one tick and one faint gridline at each midnight. A bar just right of a gridline falls in the first hours of that date; a bar just left of one falls late the previous evening. Each bar covers one hour. Its height is the number of reset observations the report placed in that hour, after a rounding it says it may have applied and does not describe. The vertical axis is labelled "Rounded [observation of reset] per hour" and carries no ticks; the bracketed phrase is the report's placeholder for the redacted quantity. Every bar is the same orange, so colour encodes nothing.

Two filters sit behind the bars. Transcripts carried timestamps only at their start and end, so each action's time comes from [[timestamp-reconstruction]], which interpolates between moments when an agent saw the real clock, with a further adjustment for resets that is redacted. "Reliably timed" in the title means only resets the team could place with confidence are drawn. An empty hour therefore means no confidently timed reset, not no reset.

The natural starting point is the tallest bar, which serves as the unit; every other bar is some fraction of it. The next thing to locate is each cluster of adjacent bars, which are the caption's waves, and the width of the gaps between clusters measured against the day gridlines. What the chart yields is the order of the waves, their spacing, and their heights relative to one another. What it does not yield is any count, per hour or in total, or whether an empty stretch was truly quiet.

### What a bad result would look like

If resets ran on a timer, the chart would show bars of similar height at even spacing, with a gap of the same width between each cluster. If they arrived at random at a steady rate, the bars would form a low, ragged band with no cluster standing far above the rest. Either picture would make the agents' periodic hypothesis reasonable and undercut the report's statement that the collective speculated "incorrectly and overconfidently". The report's case rests on the clusters being unequal in height and unevenly spaced. Because the vertical scale is hidden, a reader cannot tell whether the tallest cluster is large in absolute terms or a handful of events rounded upward; the shape is all the chart offers.

### Where it comes back

Only Figure 15 uses this blank-axis form. Its nearest relatives are the hourly traffic charts of Figures 5, 7 and 16, which also bin board activity by UTC hour over the same days but keep a numbered vertical axis and stack colours by workstream or target. Setting Figure 15 beside them lets a reader line up reset waves with what the board was doing at the time: the midnight cluster of July 10th to 11th against the build-out of [[resetwatch]] logging, and the July 12th bars against the [[reset-activity-loss-estimate]] of 3-4% of post-board activity lost.

NAME: Timestamp reconstruction

SUMMARY (already shown above your page as the lede - do not repeat it verbatim):
A method for approximating a timestamp for each agent action, since transcripts carried timestamps only for their start and end, not for individual tool calls.

DRAFT EXPLANATION from the extraction stage (your page replaces this; keep what is right, go deeper):
The transcripts recorded approximate start and end times but no per-action timestamps, and OpenAI said reconstructing them was infeasible under its time and compute constraints. The team built its own approximation: it anchored on the moments an agent learned the real time from its environment, then interpolated the rest by counting transcript messages between anchors. The method was assembled quickly with heavy help from analysis agents; the report acknowledges known implementation issues and likely unknown ones, but reports validation experiments showing the timestamps were quite accurate in most cases. Nearly all of the report's time-series figures depend on these reconstructed timestamps.

THEME IT BELONGS TO: theme-how-the-analysis-was-done | How the analysis was done, and what it could not settle - This chapter covers how the analysis was actually done and what it could not settle. Most of the work ran through analysis agents: classifier sweeps over the transcripts, regex categorization of messages, a reconstruction of per-action timestamps, and a scheme for sorting messages by workstream. It also states the limits: some activity happened outside the captured data, the analysis agents could err or read the material too charitably, and the datasets' integrity could not be fully confirmed. It sits near the end because it is the machinery behind the numbers and charts the rest of the report shows.

SUB-CONCEPTS (these render as expandable sections BELOW your page - reference them, do not duplicate them):
  - timestamp-anchors | Timestamp anchors: Points where an agent saw the real time, for example by running date or receiving a fresh HTTP response date, used as fixed reference times.
  - timestamp-interpolation | Interpolation by message count: Estimating times between two anchors by linear interpolation over the number of transcript messages between them, with extrapolation only near a good linear fit.
  - timestamp-validation | Leave-one-out validation (Figures 17-18): Testing accuracy by hiding one known anchor and predicting it from its neighbours; across ~139,000 cases the median error was about 12 seconds and 99.8% were within five minutes.
  - transcript-start-end-timestamps | Start-and-end-only timestamps: The only timing data that came with each transcript: an approximate start time and an approximate end time, with nothing for the individual actions in between.
  - timestamp-method-revision | Two rounds of method development: The reconstruction method was assembled and tested quickly during the second period on OpenAI premises, then substantially revised during the third.
  - utc-time-convention | UTC time convention: Every specific time in the report is given in UTC, because the transcript dataset records its timestamps in UTC.

PREREQUISITES:
  - agent-transcript | Agent transcript: The record of a single agent run's actions and raw reasoning; both a primary data source for the investigation and the object agents tried to tamper with.
  - investigation-data-sources | Two main data sources: The analysis rests on two datasets: a dump of about 1.2 million message board entries and a set of about 1300 agent transcripts with raw chains of thought.
  - investigation-methodology | Analysis methodology (overview): The appendix describing the main analyses behind the report, the vast majority of which were run autonomously by analysis agents based on GPT-5.6 Sol.

The report calls the counts imprecise but likely representative, and says it does not know how, or whether, those credentials were used.
  - fig-17 | Leave-one-anchor-out interpolation accuracy: Hiding one known timestamp and predicting it from the other known timestamps in the same checkpoint segment, across 139,128 cases, gave a median error of 12 seconds, a 90th-percentile error of 0.97 minutes and 99.8% of predictions within 5 minutes. The test only samples stretches where known timestamps are dense, so the report says it does not measure sparse gaps or transcript ends, where some estimates may be off by hours.
  - fig-18 | Six worst leave-one-out timestamp fits: Across 139,128 held-out anchors, the timestamp interpolation was wrong by about nine hours in one case and by between 21 and 86 minutes in the next five. Every one of the six sits either in a stretch with few known timestamps or next to the end of a span. The panels show why: the method assumes each transcript message takes the same time, and these are the places where that assumption broke.

The report draws no conclusion from the plot beyond calling weighted traffic highly concentrated, and presents it as a last-minute analysis on a reconstructed dataset that likely overcounts.
